On 7 January 1610, in the first hour after sunset, the professor of mathematics at Padua turned his best telescope on Jupiter and noticed three small, bright stars beside it, strung out in a straight line with the planet: two to the east, one to the west.[^galileo1610] Galileo Galilei took them for ordinary background stars and hardly bothered to note where they sat. The next night all three were west of Jupiter and closer together. The night after that was cloudy. On the 10th there were only two, both on the east side.

Planets do not hop back and forth like that. The little stars were moving, and they were moving with Jupiter. By 13 January Galileo had seen four of them, and by the 15th he had worked out that they were going round the planet.[^galileoproject] Two months later his pamphlet *Sidereus Nuncius* came off the press in Venice, announcing "four PLANETS, never seen from the very beginning of the world up to our own times".[^galileo1610]

The four were the first of many. As of September 2026 the official list counts 455 moons around the other planets, 293 of them Saturn's.[^jpldisc] Most are lumps a few kilometres wide. A handful turned out to be worlds in their own right: the most volcanic place in the Solar System, oceans of salt water under ice, a moon with methane rain and seas, and another that sprays its ocean into space. Here is how we found them, and the physics that makes them behave so strangely.

## Four stars that would not stay put

By Galileo's own account, his telescope made things look more than thirty times nearer than to the naked eye.[^galileo1610] At their brightest, Jupiter's four big moons shine at about magnitude 4.6 (Ganymede) to 5.8 (Callisto), faint but not hopeless;[^horizonsmag] it is the glare of Jupiter beside them that hides them.[^galileoproject]

The discovery landed in the middle of the fight over Copernicus. One stock objection to a moving Earth was the Moon: if Earth really raced round the Sun once a year, how could it keep its Moon? Galileo now had four moons that plainly kept pace with Jupiter, which everyone agreed was moving, and he pressed the point hard in his book.[^galileo1610]

Galileo needed patrons, so he named the moons the Medicean Stars after the family of Cosimo II de' Medici, Grand Duke of Tuscany.[^vanhelden1994] In Ansbach, Simon Marius claimed to have seen them first, on 29 December 1609, but that date is in the old Julian calendar; in Galileo's Gregorian calendar it was 8 January 1610, a day after Galileo.[^lindahallmarius] Marius lost the priority fight but won the naming: in his *Mundus Iovialis* of 1614 he passed on Johannes Kepler's suggestion, made at the Regensburg fair in October 1613, to name them after the lovers of Jupiter. The first would be Io, the second Europa, "the Third, on account of its majesty of light, Ganymede", and the fourth Callisto.[^vanhelden1994] The names did not catch on. Galileo numbered the moons outwards from Jupiter in his notebooks, though never in print, and for more than two centuries astronomers simply used numbers, until the names were revived in the mid-nineteenth century.[^galileoproject][^vanhelden1994] Kepler also gave us the word satellite.[^galileoproject]

::: myth Jupiter's moons proved that Earth goes round the Sun.
They did not. A system in which Jupiter and its moons circle the Sun while the Sun circles a fixed Earth fits the same observations. What the moons did was kill two arguments against Copernicus: that there could be only one centre of motion, and that Earth alone had a moon. The historian Albert Van Helden puts it plainly: no proof, but useful ammunition for the Copernican side.[^galileoproject]
:::

The moons also became a clock for finding longitude, though getting the tables good enough took almost two centuries.[^galileoproject] Along the way, timing Io showed that light takes minutes to cross the Solar System, a story told in [Light takes time](#/learn/light-takes-time).

:::

## Saturn's crowd, and a moon with a dark face

On 25 March 1655 the Dutch astronomer Christiaan Huygens pointed a new telescope at Saturn, meaning to study its puzzling rings, and found a moon instead.[^esahuygens][^nasatitan] He called it simply Luna Saturni, the moon of Saturn.[^nasatitan] Four years later his *Systema Saturnium* explained Saturn's changing shape as a thin, flat ring seen from different angles.[^galileoproject2]

The next four Saturnian moons came from Giovanni Domenico Cassini, whom Louis XIV had brought from Bologna to run the new Paris Observatory.[^esacassini] In late October 1671, with a 17-foot telescope by Giuseppe Campani, he spotted a faint new planet near Saturn and followed it from 25 October to 6 November. Its greatest distance from Saturn was three times that of Huygens's moon, so, by the rule Kepler had found for the planets, its orbit should take about five times as long.[^cassini1673] Kepler's rule says the square of the period goes as the cube of the distance, and $3^{1.5} = 5.2$. The real ratio for Iapetus and Titan is 4.98.[^nssdcsaturn] Not bad for a first fortnight.

Then Iapetus did something odd. Cassini could see it west of Saturn but lost it to the east. He concluded that one hemisphere must be far darker than the other, and that the moon keeps the same face towards Saturn. Voyager's pictures in 1980 and 1981 showed he was right on both counts.[^nasaiapetus] Cassini went on to find Rhea in 1672 and Tethys and Dione in 1684, spotted the gap in Saturn's ring that bears his name in 1675, and suggested that the ring was a swarm of tiny moonlets, close to the modern picture.[^esacassini]

The next jump needed a bigger mirror. William Herschel found Titania and Oberon, the largest moons of Uranus, on 11 January 1787.[^nasatitania][^nasaoberon] Two years later his 40-foot reflector, paid for by King George III, picked out Enceladus and Mimas, which Herschel said nobody could have expected to find with anything smaller.[^nasaenceladus][^nasamimas][^herschel1790] Saturn's new moons sat inside the old ones, and the numbering became a mess. In 1847 Herschel's son John proposed names instead, choosing Titans because "As Saturn devoured his children, his family could not be assembled round him".[^nasamimas] That is why the biggest is called Titan.

William Lassell, a brewer who spent his fortune on telescopes, found Triton on 10 October 1846, only 17 days after Neptune itself was discovered, and Ariel and Umbriel at Uranus in 1851.[^lindahalllassell][^nasaariel] The planet-hunting half of that story is in [Clockwork and chaos](#/learn/clockwork-and-chaos). In 1944 Gerard Kuiper showed that Titan has an atmosphere.[^kuiper1944]

## Fear and Dread

Jonathan Swift gave Mars two moons in 1726. In *Gulliver's Travels* the astronomers of the flying island of Laputa have found "two lesser stars, or satellites" circling Mars, 3 and 5 Mars diameters from its centre, with periods of 10 and 21.5 hours.[^swift1726] Swift even made them obey Kepler's rule: $(21.5/10)^2 = 4.62$ and $(5/3)^3 = 4.63$.

Real ones had to wait for the close approach of 1877, when Mars looked as big in a telescope as it ever gets. On the night of 11 August, urged on by his wife, the mathematician Angeline Stickney, Asaph Hall glimpsed a faint point near Mars through the 26-inch refractor of the US Naval Observatory in Washington. Bad weather set in (the observatory stood in a district called Foggy Bottom, for good reason), and only on 16 August did he find it again. The next night, waiting for it to come round, he found a second moon closer in.[^hall1877][^lindahallhall][^nasaphobos] He named them Phobos and Deimos, fear and dread, after the sons of the Greek war god.[^nasadeimos] The biggest crater on Phobos, almost 10 km across, is called Stickney.[^nasaphobos]

Swift's moons were far too slow. Phobos orbits only 9,378 km from the centre of Mars, less than one and a half Mars diameters, and goes round in 7 hours 39 minutes; Deimos takes 30.3 hours.[^nssdcmars] Phobos goes round faster than Mars spins (a Martian day is 24.6 hours), so from the ground it rises in the west, sets in the east, and comes round again about every 11 hours.

### Weighing a planet with a moon

Cassini used Kepler's rule as a pattern. Newton turned it into a set of scales. A moon is falling towards its planet all the time, and the planet's gravity supplies exactly the pull needed to bend its path into a closed orbit. A heavier planet has to be circled faster at the same distance. Put that together and the planet's mass drops out:

$$M = \frac{4\pi^2 a^3}{G\,T^2}$$

In words: the mass of the planet equals four times pi squared times the cube of the moon's orbital radius, divided by the gravitational constant times the square of the orbital period. The moon's own mass does not appear, as long as it is small compared with the planet.

Try it on Phobos. With $a = 9.378\times10^{6}$ m, $a^3 = 8.248\times10^{20}$ m³, and four pi squared times that is $3.256\times10^{22}$. The period is 0.31891 days, or 27,554 s; squared and multiplied by $G = 6.674\times10^{-11}$, that gives 0.05067. Dividing, $M = 6.43\times10^{23}$ kg, within 0.2% of the accepted mass of Mars.[^nssdcmars] Swift's inner moon, 20,300 km out with a 10-hour period, would need a Mars six times heavier. Any moon a telescope can follow is a free weighing machine for its planet, which turned out to matter a great deal for Pluto.

## Tides: the stretch that locks and heats

Why are there two high tides a day and not one? Because gravity weakens with distance. The Moon pulls the side of Earth facing it a little harder than it pulls Earth's centre, and pulls the centre a little harder than the far side. The net effect is a stretch along the Earth–Moon line, raising one bulge towards the Moon and another on the opposite side.[^noaabulges] It is the difference in pull across a body, not the pull itself, that makes a tide. For a body of radius $R$ at distance $d$ from a mass $M$, that difference is close to

$$\Delta a \approx \frac{2\,G M R}{d^3}$$

In words: the extra acceleration of the near side compared with the centre is twice the gravitational constant, times the mass doing the pulling, times the radius of the body being stretched, divided by the cube of the distance between them.

For the Moon on Earth, $M = 7.346\times10^{22}$ kg, $R = 6.371\times10^{6}$ m and $d = 3.844\times10^{8}$ m. The top line is $2 \times 6.674\times10^{-11} \times 7.346\times10^{22} \times 6.371\times10^{6} = 6.25\times10^{19}$, the bottom line is $5.68\times10^{25}$, and the answer is $1.1\times10^{-6}$ m/s², about one nine-millionth of the gravity you feel standing up. Acting on whole oceans, that is enough. Do the same sum for the Sun and you get $5.1\times10^{-7}$ m/s², less than half the Moon's, even though the Sun's overall pull on Earth is 179 times stronger. The cube of the distance wins.[^noaacause]

Run the arithmetic the other way and you see why we only ever see one face of the Moon. Earth is 81 times more massive than the Moon, so its stretch on the Moon is 22 times stronger. When the young Moon spun faster, its bulge, slow to respond, was always carried a little past the line to Earth. Earth's pull on it, and the flexing that turned spin into heat, slowed the Moon until one rotation took exactly one orbit. Then the bulge stops moving through the rock, and nothing is left to slow.[^nasatidal] All the large moons in the Solar System are locked this way, the bigger ones within hundreds of thousands of orbits.[^nasatidal]

::: myth The Moon does not rotate, and its far side is always dark.
The Moon spins once on its axis in the same 27.3 days it takes to go round Earth, which is exactly why one face stays towards us.[^nasatidal] A moon that did not rotate would show us every side over a month. The hidden hemisphere is the far side, not the dark side: at new Moon it is in full sunshine, and like Earth the Moon has a day side and a night side that change as it turns.[^nasaphases]
:::

The process is still running. Our ocean bulges are carried slightly ahead of the Moon by Earth's faster spin; their pull tugs the Moon into a wider orbit while friction slows Earth down. Laser ranging to reflectors left by Apollo shows the Moon moving away by about 3.8 cm a year.[^nssdcmoon][^dickey1994] Tidal friction alone should lengthen the day by 2.3 milliseconds per century; ancient eclipse records show 1.8, with other effects making up the difference.[^stephenson2016]

::: see-it moon-month
A month over Earth, squeezed into about 25 seconds. Keep your eye on the dark patches of the Moon's face: they never turn away from Earth, even as the Moon completes a full circle and a full spin.
:::

Move the calculation to Jupiter. Io, 3,643 km across, orbits 421,800 km from a planet 318 times as massive as Earth. Jupiter's stretch on Io comes to $6.2\times10^{-3}$ m/s², about 5,600 times the Moon's stretch on Earth.[^nssdcjovian] Io is locked, so on a perfectly circular orbit its bulge would sit still. But Io's orbit is not quite circular, and that small flaw turned out to be the most important fact about it.

## The moon that erupted

On 2 March 1979 the journal *Science* published a short paper by Stanton Peale, Patrick Cassen and R. T. Reynolds. Io's orbit, they pointed out, is forced into a slight oval by the other big moons, so its distance from Jupiter changes through each 42-hour orbit and its tidal bulge flexes in and out. They estimated the heat that flexing releases and concluded it could have melted much of Io's interior. Their abstract ended with a prediction: "Consequences of a largely molten interior may be evident in pictures of Io's surface returned by Voyager I."[^peale1979]

Voyager 1 passed Jupiter three days later, at 12:05 UT on 5 March 1979.[^nasav1] On Friday 9 March, Linda Morabito, a navigation engineer at the Jet Propulsion Laboratory, was processing a picture of Io taken to fix the spacecraft's position against the background stars. Off the edge of Io's disc stood a faint cloud, an eruption from the volcano now called Pele. In the same frame an eruption from Loki showed as a bright spot on the line between day and night.[^morabitosite] The team's one-page report called it the first evidence of active volcanism on any body other than Earth.[^morabito1979] Peale's group had published with three days to spare.

Io's heat has since been measured from its infrared glow: at least 2.5 W for every square metre.[^veeder1994] Over Io's $4.2\times10^{13}$ m² that is more than $1\times10^{14}$ W, over twice the 47 TW that leaks out of the whole Earth, from 8% of Earth's surface area.[^davies2010] It is the most volcanically active world in the Solar System, with hundreds of volcanoes and lakes of molten rock.[^nasaio]

NASA's Juno spacecraft flew about 1,500 km over Io on 30 December 2023 and again on 3 February 2024.[^junoio] By tracking how Io's gravity flexes as it swings round Jupiter, the team measured how much Io squishes: too little for a shallow global ocean of magma. Io seems to have a mostly solid mantle, with melt rising through it fast enough to erupt rather than pool.[^park2025]

## One, two, four

Why is Io's orbit an oval at all? Tides slowly make a lone moon's orbit circular, so something must keep squeezing it. That something is timing. Io goes round Jupiter in 1.769 days, Europa in 3.551 and Ganymede in 7.155: each period almost exactly double the one before.[^nssdcjovian] So Io catches up with Europa every 3.525 days, almost exactly two Io orbits, and the two meet at nearly the same point of Io's orbit every time. Small tugs repeated at the same place add up, like well-timed pushes on a swing. The kicks hold Io's orbit slightly oval, with an eccentricity of 0.004, and Europa's at 0.009.[^nssdcjovian]

The three are locked together more tightly than a simple 1:2:4 suggests. Count laps per day instead of days per lap, and this combination comes out as zero:

$$\frac{1}{P_\text{Io}} - \frac{3}{P_\text{Europa}} + \frac{2}{P_\text{Ganymede}} = 0$$

In words: Io's laps per day, minus three times Europa's laps per day, plus twice Ganymede's laps per day, add up to nothing.

With the measured periods, $1/1.769138 = 0.565247$, $3/3.551181 = 0.844789$ and $2/7.154553 = 0.279542$, and $0.565247 - 0.844789 + 0.279542 = -0.00000008$ laps per day: zero to the precision of the periods. This is the Laplace relation, named after the French mathematician Pierre-Simon Laplace.[^peale2002] Apply the same recipe to the moons' angles around Jupiter and the result stays close to 180°; if all three lined up on one side of Jupiter, it would be zero. Positions from NASA's JPL Horizons system for the month from 25 September 2026 keep it within about 4° of 180°.[^horizons] The three can never line up together.

How the moons fell into step is still argued over: perhaps tides pushed Io outwards until it caught Europa and then Ganymede, or perhaps the lock was set while the moons were forming in a disc of gas around the young Jupiter.[^peale2002] Precise measurements of their positions show the system is not frozen: Io is drifting slowly inwards, and the three are easing out of the exact relation.[^lainey2009]

Enceladus goes round Saturn twice for each orbit of Dione, and Dione's regular tug keeps Enceladus's orbit slightly oval and its insides warm.[^nasaenceladus] Pluto's little moons Styx, Nix and Hydra are tied in a three-way resonance like Io's.[^showalter2015]

### The moon that tumbles

Resonance can also breed chaos. Hyperion, a potato about 360 by 270 by 210 km, goes round Saturn three times for every four orbits of Titan (periods of 21.28 and 15.95 days), and Titan keeps Hyperion's orbit stretched.[^nssdcsaturn][^nasahyperion] In 1984 Jack Wisdom, Stanton Peale and François Mignard showed that an elongated moon on such an orbit cannot settle into a steady spin; it should tumble unpredictably.[^wisdom1984] It does. Hyperion is also barely more than half as dense as water, probably because it is riddled with empty space, and Cassini found a surface like a sponge.[^nasahyperion][^thomas2007]

## Oceans under the ice

A few months after predicting Io's volcanoes, Cassen, Reynolds and Peale turned to Europa, which is also squeezed by the resonance and is covered in water ice. Their answer, in September 1979: "It is possible that tidal dissipation in an ice crust on Europa preserved a liquid water layer beneath it", provided the resonance was ancient.[^cassen1979]

Voyager 2 flew past Europa on 9 July 1979, at 205,720 km.[^nasav2] Its pictures showed a bright surface with almost no impact craters and a web of dark bands. Some bands had sides that matched like pieces of a jigsaw, as if the crust had cracked, pulled apart and been filled from below.[^nasaeuropaocean] A young, cracked surface fits an ocean. It does not prove one.

The best evidence came from a magnetometer. NASA's Galileo spacecraft, launched from the Space Shuttle Atlantis on 18 October 1989, orbited Jupiter from 7 December 1995 and made 35 close passes of Jupiter's moons, 11 of them at Europa.[^nasagalileo] Jupiter's magnetic field is tilted, so as the planet spins, the field sweeping past each moon keeps changing direction. A changing field drives currents in anything that conducts, and the currents make an opposing field of their own. Galileo found such answering fields at Europa and Callisto.[^khurana1998] The decisive test came on 3 January 2000, when Europa sat far south of Jupiter's magnetic equator, where the outside field pointed the other way. A permanent magnet inside Europa would not have cared; the measured field flipped, as an induced one should. The best candidate for a global conductor under the ice is salty water.[^kivelson2000][^nasaeuropaocean]

Ganymede, the largest moon in the Solar System and bigger than the planet Mercury, is the only moon with a magnetic field of its own, found by Galileo in 1996.[^kivelson1996][^nasaganymede] That field makes auroras, and in 2015 a team reported Hubble Space Telescope observations of them rocking as Jupiter's field swept past. With no ocean, the auroral ovals should swing by about 5.8°; with a salty ocean damping the swing, by about 2.2°. Hubble measured 2.0°.[^saur2015] Callisto, the most heavily cratered object in the Solar System, may hide an ocean too.[^nasacallisto]

Why is any of this water liquid, 5.2 au from the Sun, where sunlight is 27 times weaker than at Earth? Rock inside the moons holds radioactive elements that release heat, and tidal flexing adds more. Ice conducts heat poorly, so a thick shell makes a good blanket: Juno's microwave instrument, which senses temperatures below the surface, found that the cold, rigid part of Europa's ice averages about 29 km thick where it looked.[^junoeuropa] And salt lowers the freezing point of water, as anyone who has salted an icy path knows.

NASA says Europa's ocean holds about twice as much water as all of Earth's oceans.[^nasaclipperlife] How deep does that make it? A thin shell of water wrapped around a sphere has volume

$$V \approx 4\pi R^2 h$$

In words: the volume of a thin layer is the surface area of the sphere it wraps, four pi times the radius squared, times the thickness of the layer.

Earth's oceans hold $1.335\times10^{9}$ km³, so twice that is $2.67\times10^{9}$ km³.[^noaaocean] Europa's radius is 1,561 km; below, say, 30 km of ice, $R \approx 1{,}530$ km. Then $4\pi R^2 = 2.94\times10^{7}$ km², and $h = 2.67\times10^{9} \div 2.94\times10^{7} \approx 91$ km. (For so thick a layer the shortcut undercounts a little; the exact sum for a spherical shell gives about 97 km.) Earth's oceans average 3.7 km deep.[^noaadepth] Europa's would be about 25 times deeper, on a moon smaller than our own.

Galileo's mission ended on 21 September 2003, when controllers steered the spacecraft into Jupiter on purpose so that it could never crash into Europa and contaminate the ocean it had helped to find.[^nasagalileo]

## Saturn's two surprises

Kuiper's atmosphere on Titan turned out to be thick. When Voyager 1 passed Saturn on 12 November 1980, its cameras saw only haze, and its instruments found nitrogen laced with methane.[^nasav1] At the surface the air is about 95% nitrogen and 5% methane, the pressure is about 45% higher than on Earth, and the temperature is −179 °C: cold enough for water ice to behave like rock and for methane to fall as rain.[^nasatitan][^fulchignoni2005]

The Cassini–Huygens mission of NASA, ESA and the Italian space agency was built to see through that haze. It launched on 15 October 1997, reached Saturn in 2004 and released the European Huygens probe on Christmas Day.[^esafactsheet][^lebreton2005] On 14 January 2005 Huygens entered Titan's atmosphere and drifted down under parachutes for almost two and a half hours.[^lebreton2005] Its camera looked down on bright highlands cut by branching channels draining into dark, flat lowlands, the pattern of rivers on Earth, probably carved by liquid methane.[^lebreton2005][^tomasko2005] It landed on what looked like a dry riverbed, a dark floodplain covered in cobbles of water ice: the first landing on any world in the outer Solar System.[^tomasko2005][^esahuygens2015]

The liquid itself turned up the next year. On 22 July 2006 Cassini's radar swept over Titan's far north and found more than 75 patches that sent back almost no radar echo, some sitting in hollows with channels running into them: lakes.[^stofan2007] Titan is the only world besides Earth known to have standing liquid on its surface, in its case methane and ethane, and Cassini's gravity measurements showed it flexing in Saturn's tides by enough to imply a water ocean deep under the ice as well.[^nasatitan][^iess2012]

::: see-it go:titan
Titan, a golden-hazed ball larger than the planet Mercury. Under the haze are dunes, rivers and, near the north pole, seas of methane and ethane.
:::

The second surprise came from Enceladus, only about 500 km across and the most reflective body in the Solar System.[^nasaenceladus] On 17 February 2005 Cassini flew by at 1,167 km (Voyager 2, at 90,000 km in 1981, had seen nothing), and its magnetometer saw Saturn's magnetic field bend near the moon. A second pass on 9 March, at 500 km, strengthened the case; the likely culprit was water vapour.[^jplenceladus2005] The magnetometer team, led by Michele Dougherty of Imperial College London, had found something nobody had anticipated around a moon this small.[^dougherty2006] Later in 2005 Cassini's cameras photographed the source: jets of icy particles blasting out of long, warm cracks near the south pole, nicknamed the tiger stripes.[^porco2006][^nasaenceladus]

The jets leave at about 400 m/s. Most of the ice falls back as snow, but some escapes to feed Saturn's faint, wide E ring.[^nasaenceladus] Flying through the spray, Cassini sampled an ocean it could never touch. It found salty ice grains; specks of silica only 2 to 8 nanometres in radius, of a kind that forms where liquid water reacts with rock at more than about 90 °C;[^hsu2015][^nasaenceladus] molecular hydrogen, a sign of ongoing water–rock reactions on the sea floor;[^waite2017] and phosphates, with phosphorus at least 100 times more concentrated than in Earth's oceans.[^postberg2023] A slight wobble in the moon's rotation showed that the ice shell floats free of the core, so the ocean is global.[^thomas2016] Water, heat, salts, organic molecules and most of the chemical ingredients of life are all there.[^nasaenceladus]

::: see-it go:enceladus
Enceladus, small and brilliant white. Look near the south pole for the long parallel cracks, the tiger stripes, where the jets come out.
:::

Mimas, the smallest and innermost of Saturn's major moons, was supposed to be the dull one. It has a crater, Herschel, 130 km across, a third of the moon's own width, with walls 5 km high and a central peak.[^nasamimas] In 2024 Valéry Lainey and colleagues, studying tiny irregularities in Mimas's orbit in Cassini data, concluded that its old, cratered shell hides a global ocean 20 to 30 km down that is probably less than 25 million years old.[^lainey2024]

::: myth Mimas was the model for the Death Star in Star Wars.
The dates rule it out. *Star Wars* opened on 25 May 1977.[^starwars] Until the Voyagers arrived, astronomers on the ground saw Mimas as little more than a dot; the first pictures of its giant crater came from Voyager 1, which reached Saturn that November.[^nasamimas][^nasav1][^smith1981] The resemblance is real, and it is a coincidence.
:::

Cassini ran low on fuel after 13 years at Saturn. On 15 September 2017 it was steered into the planet and burned up, for the same reason as Galileo: so it could never fall onto Enceladus or Titan.[^nasacassini]

## Tides that tear

Tides can do worse than lock and heat. Bring a moon close enough to its planet and the stretch across it beats its own gravity. The distance where that happens is called the Roche limit, after the French astronomer Édouard Roche. For a moon held together only by its own gravity it is roughly[^chandrasekhar1963][^murraydermott]

$$d \approx 2.44\,R\left(\frac{\rho_\text{planet}}{\rho_\text{moon}}\right)^{1/3}$$

In words: the danger distance is about two and a half times the planet's radius, multiplied by the cube root of the planet's density divided by the moon's density. Dense planets and fluffy moons make the danger zone bigger. (The number in front depends on how the moon deforms; 2.44 is the classic value for a liquid moon.)

For Mars, $R = 3{,}389.5$ km and the density is 3,934 kg/m³; Phobos is only 1,900 kg/m³.[^nssdcmars] The density ratio is 2.07, its cube root is 1.27, and $d \approx 2.44 \times 3{,}389.5 \times 1.27 \approx 10{,}500$ km. Phobos orbits at 9,378 km, already inside the line. Rock has some strength of its own that a liquid lacks, so Phobos survives for now, but it is closing in on Mars by about 1.8 m a century. Within roughly 50 million years it will either hit Mars or be torn into a ring.[^nasaphobos][^blackmittal2015]

For Triton the same sum gives about 55,700 km, and Triton orbits at 354,800 km, more than six times farther out.[^nssdcneptune] It is safe for now, but it is going the wrong way. Triton is the only large moon in the Solar System that orbits backwards, against its planet's spin, and it was probably a Kuiper Belt object that Neptune captured.[^nasatriton] Perhaps Triton arrived as half of a pair, like Pluto and Charon, and Neptune kept Triton while flinging its partner away.[^agnor2006] Because Triton goes backwards, the tides it raises on Neptune drag it inwards. In 1989 Christopher Chyba and colleagues estimated that it will reach Neptune's Roche limit in 1.4 to 3.6 billion years, depending on assumptions.[^chyba1989] A 2025 analysis of telescope measurements of Triton's orbit put the plunge much further off, about 28 billion years away; how fast Neptune's tides work is still poorly known.[^wang2025]

Voyager 2 flew past Neptune on 25 August 1989 and found Triton, at −235 °C one of the coldest surfaces in the Solar System, erupting.[^nasav2][^nasatriton] Voyager caught at least four geyser-like plumes; the two best documented shot dark material about 8 km up, where the wind carried it more than 100 km sideways. One explanation the Voyager team put forward was a solid greenhouse: sunlight warms nitrogen ice from below, and pressurised gas bursts out carrying dark dust. Warming the ice by less than 4 degrees above the 38 K surface would be enough.[^soderblom1990]

::: see-it go:triton
Triton, pale and icy, with few craters: its young surface has smooth plains, mounds and round pits left by icy volcanism.[^nasatriton] It is only 38 degrees above absolute zero, and still erupting.
:::

## Pluto's partner

In June 1978 James Christy and Robert Harrington of the US Naval Observatory in Flagstaff, Arizona, were measuring photographs of Pluto to refine its orbit. Christy noticed that some images looked stretched, with a bump on one side that moved around Pluto and came back every 6.39 days.[^nasacharon] Plates from 1970 and 1965 showed it too. The announcement on 7 July 1978 described a satellite about 20,000 km from Pluto and an implied ratio of the Sun's mass to Pluto's of 140,000,000 to 1.[^iauc3241] Christy named it Charon, after the ferryman of the underworld, and because it began with the first four letters of his wife Charlene's name.[^nasacharon]

That mass ratio was the headline. In 1930, the year Pluto was found,[^nssdcpluto] Seth Nicholson and Nicholas Mayall had used its apparent tug on Neptune to estimate its mass at $1.08 \pm 0.23$ Earth masses.[^nicholson1930] Charon settled it. Put the modern numbers into the weighing formula: $a = 1.9596\times10^{7}$ m and $T = 6.3872$ days $= 5.519\times10^{5}$ s give $M = 1.46\times10^{22}$ kg for Pluto and Charon together,[^nssdcpluto] about one four-hundredth of Earth's mass. Pluto's story continues in [The edges of the Solar System](#/learn/edges-of-the-solar-system).

Charon is 1,212 km across, just over half Pluto's width, with 12% of its mass; our own Moon has 1.2% of Earth's, and Titan only 0.024% of Saturn's.[^nssdcpluto][^nssdcmoon][^nssdcsaturn] The point they both circle, their centre of mass, lies about 2,100 km from Pluto's centre, outside Pluto altogether. Each has locked the other, so Pluto and Charon always keep the same faces towards each other.[^nasacharon] NASA's New Horizons flew past on 14 July 2015 and showed Charon with a reddish north polar region.[^nasacharon] Pluto's four small moons, found between 2005 and 2012, are pushed around by the swinging pair: Nix and Hydra tumble chaotically.[^jpldisc][^showalter2015]

:::

## What comes next

### JUICE, flying past Earth this week

ESA's Jupiter Icy Moons Explorer launched on 14 April 2023. After swinging past the Moon and Earth in 2024 and Venus in 2025, on 28 September 2026 it passes 8,640 km above the Indian Ocean for a boost of about 3.5 km/s.[^esajuiceflyby] A last Earth flyby in January 2029 sends it to Jupiter in July 2031. After 35 flybys of the big moons it will slow into orbit around Ganymede in 2034, the first spacecraft to orbit a moon other than our own.[^esajuice][^esaganymede]

**Status:** cruising. Jupiter arrival July 2031; Ganymede orbit 2034.[^esajuice]

### Europa Clipper

NASA's Europa Clipper, more than 30 m across with its solar arrays out, left on a Falcon Heavy on 14 October 2024. After a Mars flyby in 2025 it swings past Earth on 3 December 2026 and reaches Jupiter in April 2030, to make 49 close flybys of Europa with nine instruments and a gravity experiment.[^nasaclipper] It and JUICE will work at Jupiter at the same time.[^esajuice]

**Status:** cruising. Jupiter arrival April 2030.[^nasaclipper]

### MMX, a sample from Phobos

Japan's Martian Moons eXploration mission is scheduled to launch on an H3 rocket from Tanegashima on 20 October 2026. It should reach Mars in 2027, land on Phobos and bring more than 10 g of it home.[^mmx] The sample should settle whether Phobos is a captured asteroid or debris thrown up when something hit Mars.[^mmx]

**Status:** launch scheduled for 20 October 2026, though launch dates can slip; sample return planned for Japan's 2031 fiscal year.[^mmx]

### Dragonfly, a drone for Titan

Dragonfly is a nuclear-powered rotorcraft being built at the Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory. It will hop across Titan for 3.3 years, sampling organic dunes and the 80 km crater Selk, where liquid water and organic chemicals may once have mixed. In September 2026 NASA announced that the IAU had approved a name for its landing region, a dune field south of Selk: Ahmakiq Undae.[^dragonfly2026]

**Status:** in assembly and testing. Launch no earlier than July 2028; arrival at Titan late 2034.[^nasadragonfly]

### Back to Enceladus, eventually

The US planetary science decadal survey for 2023 to 2032 ranked an Enceladus Orbilander, which would sample the plume from orbit and then land for two years, as its second-priority new flagship mission after a Uranus orbiter and probe. Started late in this decade, it would arrive in the early 2050s.[^decadal2022][^mackenzie2021] In 2021 ESA chose "moons of the giant planets" as the theme for one of its large missions between 2035 and 2050, possibly with a lander or a drone.[^voyage2050]

**Status:** studies only. Neither mission had a launch date as of September 2026, and funding for both is uncertain.

### Open questions

Nobody yet knows whether anything lives in the oceans of Europa or Enceladus, whether ocean water ever reaches Europa's surface, or when the Laplace resonance formed. The missions above were designed to find out.
