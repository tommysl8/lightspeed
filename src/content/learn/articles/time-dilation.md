Near the top of Mount Washington in New Hampshire, in a laboratory 6,265 ft (about 1,910 m) up, David Frisch and James Smith of MIT slid a plastic cylinder under a pile of iron bars whose roof alone weighed 10 tons. The iron was a speed filter. Cosmic-ray muons arriving at less than 0.9950 of the speed of light were stopped by the 2½ ft of iron. Muons faster than 0.9954 went straight through the iron and the plastic. The ones in between came to rest inside the plastic and, a couple of microseconds later, decayed, leaving two flashes on an oscilloscope: one when the muon arrived and one when it died. With the cameras running, 568 muons stopped and died in one hour.[^frischsmith1963]

Then they moved the apparatus to Cambridge, Massachusetts, 10 ft above sea level, took a foot of iron off the pile to make up for the air the muons would now have to cross, and counted again. A muon at 0.9952 of the speed of light needs 6.4 μs to fall the 1,907 m between the two stations. Stopped muons, as the mountain data showed, live about 2.2 μs on average. From their own measured decay curve, Frisch and Smith predicted that about 27 of the 568 should survive the fall. The counter at sea level recorded 412.[^frischsmith1963]

The counter was fine. The muons had lived through less time than the clocks in Cambridge said had passed: about 0.7 μs instead of 6.4. That is time dilation. A clock moving relative to you runs slow, by an amount you can work out in advance, and this is one of the most thoroughly tested statements in physics. Einstein predicted it in 1905. Nobody caught it until 1938, and by 2022 physicists could see gravity's version of it across a single millimetre.

## A factor waiting for a meaning

The slowing factor existed before anyone thought it had to do with time. Joseph Larmor, a Cambridge mathematical physicist, was the first to use the transformations now named after Lorentz.[^mactutor] In his 1897 theory of electrons moving through the aether they came with a side effect: electrons circling inside a moving body should complete their orbits more slowly, by exactly the factor that now carries the Greek letter γ (gamma).[^larmor1897] Hendrik Lorentz arrived at the same equations around the same time. For both men it described matter pushing through the aether, not time itself. Larmor never came round to the other reading; in the end he rejected even special relativity.[^mactutor]

Einstein's 1905 paper on the electrodynamics of moving bodies made the factor about time itself. There is no aether in it, only two assumptions: the laws of physics look the same to everyone moving steadily, and everyone measures the same speed for light. In section 4 he spells out what that means for a clock. Take two synchronised clocks, carry one of them round a closed path at speed $v$ for a time $t$ and bring it back, and it will be behind the one that stayed put by $\tfrac{1}{2}tv^2/c^2$ seconds, for speeds well below that of light. Then he offered a concrete case: "a balance-clock at the equator must go more slowly" than an identical clock at one of the poles.[^einstein1905] The principle was right. The example was wrong, for a reason his later theory of gravity would explain.

In 1911 the French physicist Paul Langevin, speaking at a philosophy congress in Bologna, gave the idea the form everyone remembers. His traveller agrees to be locked in a projectile fired from Earth at a speed just one twenty-thousandth below the speed of light. He flies out for one year of his own time, turns round and comes back. He has aged two years. The Earth has aged two hundred.[^langevin1911] There were no twins in Langevin's version. They arrived later, and so did the word paradox.

Then for decades nobody could test it, because at reachable speeds the effect is tiny. At 30 m/s, a fast car, a moving clock runs slow by 5 parts in $10^{15}$; at the 250 m/s of a jet airliner, by 3.5 parts in $10^{13}$, about 30 billionths of a second a day. No clock of 1911 came close. The first people to catch time dilation used atoms and particles, which carry their own clocks.

## Atoms as clocks

An atom that gives off light is a clock of sorts: its light oscillates at a frequency fixed by the atom's structure. If the atom's internal clock slows when it moves, the frequency of its light drops by the factor γ, on top of the ordinary Doppler shift from its motion towards or away from you. In 1907 Einstein suggested looking for exactly this in a canal-ray tube, where a high voltage fires glowing ions down a glass tube.[^einstein1907]

The trouble is size. The ordinary Doppler shift grows in proportion to $v/c$, while the time-dilation shift grows with $(v/c)^2$ and is far smaller. Herbert Ives and G. R. Stilwell of Bell Telephone Laboratories got round this in 1938 with a mirror. They accelerated hydrogen ions with voltages of up to about 20,000 V, to around 0.005 of the speed of light, and placed a small concave mirror inside the tube so that their spectrograph saw the light twice: once from atoms moving towards it and once, by reflection, from atoms moving away. The two ordinary Doppler shifts are equal and opposite, so their midpoint should sit exactly on the wavelength of a hydrogen atom at rest. If moving atoms run slow, the midpoint sits slightly to the red. Near 20,000 V the ordinary Doppler shift moved the lines about 2 mm across their photographic plates; the displacement they were hunting was about 0.05 ångström, or 0.005 mm, and they measured the plates with a Zeiss microscope that read to 0.001 mm. They found it, at the right size, growing with voltage the way the formula said it should.[^ives1938]

The twist is in the conclusion. Ives did not accept Einstein's reading of relativity. The paper frames the change of clock rate as part of the Larmor–Lorentz aether theory from its first paragraph, declares that theory verified, and closes by calling the result more decisive evidence for it than the null experiments had given.[^ives1938] The first laboratory measurement of time dilation was made by a man who took it as evidence for the aether Einstein had thrown out.[^frischsmith1963]

In 2014 a team at the GSI storage ring in Darmstadt reported an Ives–Stilwell experiment with lithium ions circulating at 0.338 of the speed of light and confirmed the relativistic link between speed and γ to within 2.3 parts in a billion.[^botermann2014]

## Clocks that fall out of the sky

Cosmic rays, mostly protons accelerated somewhere in our galaxy, hit the top of the atmosphere and make showers of short-lived particles.[^frischsmith1963] Among them are muons, heavier cousins of the electron with about 207 times its mass, which at rest decay with a mean life of 2.197 μs.[^pdg] In 1940 they were still called mesotrons, and they posed a puzzle: a layer of air absorbed far more of them than a layer of solid matter of the same mass.[^rossihall1941] The favoured explanation was that they decay in flight: in thin air, crossing a given mass takes a long path and a long time.

If that was right, relativity made a sharp prediction. A muon's chance of decaying per metre travelled should be inversely proportional to its momentum, because a faster muon covers the distance sooner and also carries a slower clock. Bruno Rossi and David Hall of the University of Chicago set out to check the decay idea, and wrote that they meant to test the relativistic formula for time intervals at the same time. They put Geiger counters and slabs of lead in a moving van and drove it between Denver, at 1,616 m, and Echo Lake, 3,240 m up and about 30 miles to the west. By adding or removing 115 g/cm² of lead they split the muons into a slower group and a faster group. The slower group disintegrated about three times faster than the faster one, as the relativistic formula predicted, and their numbers gave a lifetime at rest of (2.4 ± 0.3) μs.[^rossihall1941] The modern value is 2.1969811 μs.[^pdg] An American Physical Society plaque now marks the site and credits the experiments there with the first measurement of the muon lifetime and a confirmation of Einstein's time dilation.[^apsecho]

### Mount Washington, on film

Rossi and Hall's result was solid but indirect: it lived inside counting rates and absorber thicknesses. Twenty-two years later Frisch and Smith wanted something a student could watch. Their film, *Time Dilation: An Experiment with Mu-Mesons*, was planned with Francis Friedman as part of a series on special relativity at MIT's Science Teaching Center, paid for by the National Science Foundation and produced by Educational Services Incorporated.[^frischsmith1963][^film1962] James Smith's permanent address, the paper notes, was the physics department of the University of Illinois at Urbana.

Building for the camera cost precision: they skipped a second counter that would have rejected steeply slanting muons, to keep the experiment simple for filming. They repeated the hour-long count six times on the mountain and five times at sea level, and the averages came out at 563 ± 10 and 408 ± 9 per hour. One mountain count of 559 was, they admitted in print, reported as 570 in the film.[^frischsmith1963]

The muons do not keep a steady speed as they fall. They lose energy in the air, so their dilation factor drops from about 10.2 at the top to about 6.8 at the bottom. Averaging this properly gave a predicted factor of 8.4 ± 2. The counts gave 8.8 ± 0.8.[^frischsmith1963]

### Counting survivors

Radioactive decay has no memory. A muon that has already lasted 3 μs is no more likely to decay in the next microsecond than a fresh one. That gives the half-life rule:

$$
N = N_0 \times \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{t/t_{1/2}}
$$

In words: start with $N_0$ particles; after one half-life $t_{1/2}$ half of them remain, after two half-lives a quarter, and in general you multiply by one half once for every half-life that passes, fractions of a half-life included.

For a muon the half-life is its mean life times 0.693, which is 1.52 μs. Frisch and Smith's muons took 6.4 μs to fall, and 6.4 ÷ 1.52 = 4.2 half-lives. Two to the power 4.2 is about 18, so without time dilation roughly 568 ÷ 18 ≈ 31 muons should reach sea level, close to the 27 they read off their measured curve. Now let the muons' clocks run slow by a factor of 9. They advance only 6.4 ÷ 9 ≈ 0.7 μs, less than half a half-life, and $(1/2)^{0.47} \approx 0.72$. That predicts 0.72 × 568 ≈ 410 survivors. They counted 412.

A longer fall makes it lopsided. A muon made 15 km up at 0.995 of the speed of light needs about 50 μs to reach the ground: 33 half-lives, and $(1/2)^{33}$ is about one in ten billion. With γ = 10 its own clock advances only 5 μs, 3.3 half-lives, and about one in ten gets through. Time dilation turns one survivor into a billion.

## One number: γ

All of these experiments come down to a single number. Relativity keeps two kinds of time apart. Proper time, written $\tau$ (tau), is what a clock records along its own path: the muon's internal clock, the atom's oscillation, your own heartbeat. Coordinate time, written $t$, is what a set of synchronised clocks at rest in some frame records between two events, such as a muon's birth high in the air and its death near the ground. For a clock moving steadily at speed $v$ through that frame, the two are related by

$$
\Delta t = \gamma\,\Delta\tau, \qquad \gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}
$$

In words: the time that passes on the frame's clocks equals the time that passes on the moving clock multiplied by gamma, and gamma is one divided by the square root of one minus the square of the speed as a fraction of the speed of light.

Take $v = 0.99c$. Then $v^2/c^2 = 0.9801$, one minus that is 0.0199, its square root is 0.141, and γ = 1 ÷ 0.141 = 7.09. A flight that takes 24 hours by Earth's clocks takes 24 ÷ 7.09 = 3.4 hours by yours. That is roughly what a trip at 0.99c to Voyager 1 would cost you, since the probe is now almost a light-day from Earth; it crosses that mark on 18 November 2026.[^voyager]

::: see-it fly:voyager1?beta=0.99
Watch the two clocks side by side. At 0.99c the home clock runs about seven times faster than the ship's, so it finishes close to a day while yours shows under three and a half hours.
:::

The formula has a long flat stretch and then a cliff. At everyday speeds γ is so close to 1 that the approximation $\gamma - 1 \approx v^2/2c^2$ is easier to use, which is where Einstein's $\tfrac{1}{2}tv^2/c^2$ came from. Past about 0.9c it climbs steeply, and it has no ceiling: as $v$ gets closer to $c$, γ grows without limit. That is the same fact, seen from another side, as the one in [Why nothing outruns light](#/learn/nothing-outruns-light).

| Speed | γ | One hour on board lasts, at home |
|---|---|---|
| 0.1c | 1.005 | 1 h 0 min 18 s |
| 0.5c | 1.155 | 1 h 9 min |
| 0.866c | 2.000 | 2 h |
| 0.9c | 2.294 | 2 h 18 min |
| 0.99c | 7.089 | 7 h 5 min |
| 0.9952c (Mount Washington muons, at the top) | 10.2 | 10 h 13 min |
| 0.999c | 22.37 | 22 h 22 min |
| 0.99942c (CERN muons) | 29.33 | 29 h 20 min |
| 0.9999c | 70.71 | 2 days 22 h 43 min |
| 0.99999c | 223.6 | 9 days 7 h 36 min |
:::

One warning. Time dilation is what is left after you allow for the time light takes to reach you. What you actually see also includes the Doppler effect, which makes an approaching clock appear to race; [What you would see near the speed of light](#/learn/seeing-near-light-speed) deals with that. The clock is still slow. It just does not look slow while it is coming at you.

## Muons going round in circles

In 1974 CERN installed its third experiment to measure the magnetism of the muon, built around a storage ring 14 m across.[^cernarchive] Muons circulated in it at γ = 29.33, about 0.99942 of the speed of light, until they decayed. In 1977 the team reported how long they lasted in the laboratory: 64.419 μs for positive muons and 64.368 μs for negative ones.[^bailey1977] Multiply the mean life at rest, 2.197 μs, by 29.33 and you get 64.44 μs. At 95% confidence the dilation factor matched the measurement to 2 parts in 1,000, and the final report two years later tightened this to (0.8 ± 0.7) × $10^{-3}$.[^bailey1979]

A muon at rest lasts about two millionths of a second. These lasted sixty-four millionths, long enough to go round a 44 m ring several hundred times. The title of the 1977 paper also hides a second test: the lifetimes were measured "in a circular orbit". To hold muons on a 7 m radius the magnet has to pull them sideways very hard; in the muons' own frame the acceleration works out at around $10^{18}$ times Earth's gravity. Their decay rate did not care. It followed their speed alone.

::: myth The twin paradox needs general relativity, because acceleration is what slows the travelling clock.
Clock rates depend on speed, not on acceleration. Muons whipped round CERN's ring at around $10^{18}$ g decayed at the rate their speed predicted, to within about one part in a thousand.[^bailey1977] Special relativity handles the twins on its own; acceleration only matters because it marks which twin changed direction, as the diagram further down shows.
:::

Decades later time dilation had become a design parameter. The Muon g−2 experiment at Fermilab used the same *magic* γ of about 29.3 in a ring of 7.112 m radius.[^g2in2021] Its muons last about 64.4 μs in the laboratory, and its final result, announced in June 2025 with a precision of 127 parts per billion, comes from fitting a decay curve with that time-dilated lifetime built into it.[^g2in2025]

## Caesium clocks in economy class

Particles are one thing; people wanted to see the effect in an actual clock. In October 1971 Joseph Hafele, a physicist at Washington University in St Louis, and Richard Keating of the US Naval Observatory did it with four commercial caesium-beam atomic clocks.[^hk1972a]

They flew them round the world on regularly scheduled airline flights, twice. The eastward trip began on 4 October and took 65.4 hours, 41.2 of them in the air; the westward trip began on 13 October and took 80.3 hours, 48.6 in the air. Flight crews from Pan Am, TWA and American Airlines recorded position, height and speed so the prediction could be worked out leg by leg, and Pan Am and TWA sent escorts along at no extra cost.[^hk1972a]

Direction matters because the Earth turns. Look down on the North Pole from a frame that does not rotate. A clock sitting on the equator is already moving east at 465 m/s. An eastbound airliner adds its own speed to that, so it moves faster than the ground clock and should lose time. A westbound airliner subtracts, moves more slowly than the ground clock and should gain. On top of that, both flew about 10 km up, where weaker gravity makes clocks run slightly fast (more on that in the next section). The predictions and results, in nanoseconds:[^hk1972a][^hk1972b]

| | Eastward | Westward |
|---|---|---|
| Speed effect, predicted | −184 ± 18 | +96 ± 10 |
| Gravity effect, predicted | +144 ± 14 | +179 ± 18 |
| Total, predicted | −40 ± 23 | +275 ± 21 |
| Measured | −59 ± 10 | +273 ± 7 |

Both results agree with the predictions inside the error bars. The second of their two papers opens with a line worth pinning above any argument about relativity: "In science, relevant experimental facts supersede theoretical arguments."[^hk1972b]

The error bars were wide for a reason. No two caesium clocks of 1971 kept exactly the same rate; in extreme cases they drifted apart by up to a microsecond a day, far more than the effect being measured, and the rates jumped now and then without warning. Flying four clocks and analysing the whole group let Hafele and Keating separate the flight from the drift.[^hk1972b]

::: myth A clock at the equator runs slower than a clock at the North Pole.
Einstein said so in 1905, reasoning from the equator's speed alone. Gravity cancels it. The Earth bulges, so a clock at the equator sits farther from the centre, higher in Earth's gravity, and runs fast by just enough to make up for its speed. All clocks at rest at sea level, on the surface geodesists call the geoid, tick at the same rate.[^ashby2003][^hk1972a]
:::

## Gravity joins in

Hafele and Keating needed a gravity row in their table because Einstein's later theory added a second way to slow a clock: put it deeper in a gravitational field. Einstein worked towards this between 1907 and 1911, when he showed that light climbing out of the Sun's gravity should arrive slightly redder.[^einstein1911] Near Earth's surface the effect amounts to about one part in $10^{16}$ per metre of height.

The first clean measurement sent gamma rays up and down the 22.5 m tower of Harvard's Jefferson Physical Laboratory in 1959 and 1960. Robert Pound and Glen Rebka used the Mössbauer effect to get a spectral line narrow enough to show a shift of a few parts in $10^{15}$, and Pound and Snider later brought the accuracy to 1%.[^pound1960][^will2014] In June 1976 Gravity Probe A carried a hydrogen maser clock on a Scout rocket from Wallops Island, Virginia, to about 10,000 km and back down into the Atlantic, a flight of 1 hour 55 minutes. Compared continuously with a maser on the ground, its rate matched Einstein's prediction at the level of 70 parts per million, the team reported; later analyses quote the limit more cautiously, at 140 to 200 parts per million.[^vessot1980][^gpa][^will2014][^delva2018]

That record stood for 42 years, and an accident beat it. In 2014 two satellites of Europe's Galileo navigation system were launched into the wrong, stretched-out orbits, so their clocks rise and fall in Earth's gravity on every orbit. Two teams used the mistake, and one of them, with 1,008 days of data, confirmed the gravitational effect to 2.5 parts in 100,000, 5.6 times better than the 140 parts per million usually quoted for Gravity Probe A.[^delva2018][^herrmann2018]

## GPS, or relativity on a budget

Every GPS satellite carries atomic clocks and broadcasts its time. A receiver that hears four satellites can work out where it is and what time it is, because each signal says when it left, and light covers about 30 cm per nanosecond. That makes clock errors expensive: a microsecond of error is 300 m of distance.[^ashby2003] ([Light takes time](#/learn/light-takes-time) has more on travel times as distances.)

The satellites orbit 26,560 km from Earth's centre at 3.87 km/s, going round about twice a day. Their speed slows their clocks relative to clocks on the ground by about 7.2 μs a day. Being higher, partly out of Earth's gravity, speeds them up by about 45.8 μs a day. The net result is that a satellite clock gains 38.6 μs a day, a fractional rate of $4.465 \times 10^{-10}$.[^ashby2003]

Not everyone in the 1970s was convinced. When NTS-2, carrying the first caesium clock put into orbit, was launched on 23 June 1977, there was doubt about the size of the correction and even its sign, and some doubted it was needed at all. So the clock was fitted with a frequency synthesiser that could apply the correction, and the synthesiser was left off for about 20 days while the clock's rate was measured. It ran fast by 442.5 parts in $10^{12}$. General relativity predicted 446.5.[^ashby2003] Satellite clocks that get the correction are now set before launch to tick at 10.229 999 995 43 MHz instead of 10.23 MHz, so that seen from the ground they tick at exactly 10.23.[^ashby2003]

Receivers apply a relativistic correction of their own, because the orbits are not perfect circles. For a satellite with an orbital eccentricity of 0.01, the clock runs fast and slow around the orbit by up to about 23 ns, which is several metres of range.[^ashby2003]

::: myth Without relativity, GPS positions would drift by about 10 km a day.
The satellite clocks would gain 38.6 μs a day, and 38.6 μs of light travel is 11.6 km, which is where the figure comes from.[^pogge] But a receiver never trusts its own cheap clock. It solves for its clock error as a fourth unknown alongside latitude, longitude and height, so an error shared by every satellite is absorbed there and leaves the position alone. The control segment also measures each satellite clock and broadcasts corrections, and by 2003 it had become common practice to launch rubidium clocks without the factory offset and fix their rate in the broadcast message instead.[^ashby2003] What would break is time: GPS time would run away from UTC by 38.6 μs a day, fatal for the many users who take their time from GPS and expect about 100 ns, and effects that differ from satellite to satellite, such as the orbit-shape term, would still cost metres.[^misra2023]
:::

## Relativity you can lift

By 2010 clocks had become good enough to bring the whole business indoors. At NIST in Boulder, Chin-Wen Chou and colleagues in David Wineland's group compared two clocks, each based on a single aluminium ion, in separate laboratories joined by 75 m of optical fibre.[^chou2010]

To test motion, they nudged the ion in one clock off the centre of its trap so that the trap's 59 MHz electric field shook it back and forth. The paper points out that the shaken ion is in effect the travelling twin, making many tiny round trips. At speeds below 10 m/s, about 36 km/h, the shaken clock fell behind by the predicted amount. Then they raised one clock by 33 cm. The higher clock ran faster by (4.1 ± 1.6) × $10^{-17}$, which works out to a height change of 37 ± 15 cm.[^chou2010]

For small heights and slow speeds, the two effects add simply:

$$
\frac{\Delta f}{f} \approx \frac{g\,h}{c^2} - \frac{v^2}{2c^2}
$$

In words: the fractional change in a clock's ticking rate is the strength of gravity times the height the clock is raised, divided by the speed of light squared, minus the square of its speed divided by twice the speed of light squared. Higher clocks run fast; moving clocks run slow.

For Chou's lift, $gh/c^2 = 9.80 \times 0.33 / (3.00 \times 10^8)^2 = 3.6 \times 10^{-17}$. A day is 86,400 s, so that is $3.1 \times 10^{-12}$ s a day, three trillionths of a second. Over a 79-year life, NIST pointed out, it adds up to about 90 billionths of a second: the extra age of someone who spends their whole life 33 cm higher than a friend.[^nist2010] The motion side is bigger. At 10 m/s, $v^2/2c^2 = 100 / (1.8 \times 10^{17}) = 5.6 \times 10^{-16}$, fifteen times the effect of the lift.

The formula works only for heights much smaller than the Earth's radius; the GPS figures above need the full gravitational potential.

Other groups took the idea outdoors and down to smaller scales. In work published in 2020, RIKEN researchers placed transportable strontium clocks near the base of the Tokyo Skytree broadcasting tower and 450 m up, and tested the gravitational effect to about one part in 10,000.[^takamoto2020] In 2022 Jun Ye's group at JILA in Boulder saw it inside a single cloud of about 100,000 strontium atoms: atoms near the top of a sample about a millimetre tall ticked faster than those near the bottom by the predicted 1.1 parts in $10^{19}$ per millimetre, measured with an uncertainty of $7.6 \times 10^{-21}$.[^bothwell2022][^nist2022] In 2023 a University of Wisconsin group did the same with five small clouds stacked over 1 cm.[^zheng2023]

This has a practical side. One centimetre of height changes a clock's rate by 1.1 parts in $10^{18}$, and the best clocks now resolve that.[^takamoto2020] Compare two clocks and you learn their difference in gravitational potential, which geodesists turn into height. This is relativistic geodesy, and it has already left the laboratory. In a campaign reported in 2018, a transportable strontium clock measured the gravity potential difference between a laboratory in the middle of a mountain and a site 90 km away.[^grotti2018]

## The twin paradox, drawn properly

Back to Langevin's traveller. The objection everyone raises is symmetry. From the traveller's seat it is the Earth that moves at nearly the speed of light, so the Earth's clocks should be the slow ones, and they cannot both be behind when the two meet again.

The way out is a diagram. Draw time running up the page and distance across it, and choose units so that one light-year across matches one year up; a flash of light then travels along a line at 45°. Each object's path is a line, its worldline. The twin who stays home traces a straight vertical line between two events, "departure" at the bottom and "reunion" at the top. The traveller's line leans to the right on the way out, bends sharply at the far star and leans back to meet the home line at the reunion: a letter V lying on its side, with its point at the star. Both lines join the same two events. The question is which line has more time along it, and the answer comes from a version of Pythagoras with a minus sign:

$$
(\Delta\tau)^2 = (\Delta t)^2 - \left(\frac{\Delta x}{c}\right)^2
$$

In words: for a straight piece of worldline, the proper time squared equals the coordinate time squared minus the square of the distance covered, where the distance is turned into a time by dividing by the speed of light.

Send the traveller at 0.8c to a star 4 light-years away and straight back. Each leg takes $\Delta t = 4 \div 0.8 = 5$ years by Earth's clocks and covers $\Delta x/c = 4$ years' worth of distance. So each leg has $\Delta\tau = \sqrt{5^2 - 4^2} = \sqrt{9} = 3$ years on the traveller's clock. Home twin: 10 years. Traveller: 6. It is the 3-4-5 triangle with one sign flipped. (With γ = 1/√(1 − 0.8²) = 1.667, 10 ÷ 1.667 = 6 as well.)

In ordinary geometry the straight line between two points is the shortest. In spacetime the straight worldline between two events has the most proper time, and every detour has less. The home twin's worldline is straight and the traveller's is bent. That is the entire asymmetry.

Why the traveller, and not the Earth? Because only one of them changes course. The home twin stays in one inertial frame throughout. The traveller uses two, one heading out and one heading back, and feels the switch between them the way you feel a train braking. While both coast, each really does see the other's clock running slow, and there is no contradiction, because they disagree about which distant events are happening "now". When the traveller turns round, her idea of the current date back on Earth jumps forward by more than six years.

A flash-counting argument that Hermann Bondi made popular in his book *Relativity and Common Sense* needs no talk of "now" at all.[^bondi1964] Suppose each twin sends a flash of light every year on their birthday. At 0.8c the Doppler effect stretches the gap between arriving flashes by a factor of 3 while the twins are separating and squeezes it by 3 while they approach. Count what each one receives:

| | Home twin receives | Traveller receives |
|---|---|---|
| Slow flashes, one every 3 years | 3, spread over the first 9 years | 1, over her first 3 years |
| Fast flashes, three a year | 3, in the last year | 9, over her last 3 years |
| Total | 6: the traveller has aged 6 | 10: home has aged 10 |

Both use the same rules and agree completely. The difference is when the switch comes. The traveller starts receiving fast flashes the moment she turns round, halfway through her trip. The home twin starts receiving them only in year 9, because the light carrying news of the turnaround takes 4 years to get home. The one who turns sees the change early.

::: myth Time dilation is an illusion caused by the time light takes to reach you.
Light delay and the Doppler effect are real, and they change what you see, but they are allowed for before anyone quotes a time dilation. The clean test is to bring the clocks back together, and when that is done the difference stays in the numbers: the flying caesium clocks came home 59 ns behind and 273 ns ahead, and the muons that reached sea level were counted there.[^hk1972b][^frischsmith1963]
:::

The app puts you in the traveller's seat, in a ship that holds 1 g, your normal weight, and turns round halfway to brake. It never coasts, so γ climbs and then falls, but the totals still come out lopsided. [Rockets to the stars](#/learn/rockets-to-the-stars) explains how such a ship would work.

::: see-it fly:proxima
A 1 g flip-and-burn flight to Proxima Centauri takes about 3.5 years on the ship's clock and 5.9 years on the home clock. Watch the gap open fastest in the middle of the flight, when the ship is going fastest.
:::

::: see-it fly:andromeda
Hold 1 g all the way to the Andromeda Galaxy, flipping at the midpoint, and your clock reads about 29 years on arrival. The home clock reads about 2.5 million. At the midpoint γ passes a million.
:::

Beyond a certain distance even unlimited γ is not enough, because the universe is expanding, and that limit is the subject of [The edge of reach](#/learn/the-edge-of-reach).

## What comes next

Time dilation is settled. The work now is in using it, and in pushing clocks to where they might show Einstein's theory needs help.

### Clocks outside the space station

ESA's Atomic Clock Ensemble in Space, ACES, went up on 21 April 2025 on a SpaceX cargo flight, was fixed to the outside of the International Space Station's Columbus module four days later and was switched on on 28 April.[^esaaces][^esaacesonline] It carries PHARAO, a caesium clock using laser-cooled atoms developed by the French space agency CNES, and a hydrogen maser built in Switzerland, about 400 km up.[^esaaces7] After six months of commissioning comes a planned two-year science phase of ten 25-day sessions, comparing the orbiting clocks with the best clocks on the ground. The headline goal is the gravitational effect to 2 or 3 parts per million, about ten times better than the Galileo result and more than twenty times better than Gravity Probe A.[^savalle2019] Status in September 2026: by the published schedule the science phase should be running, but no science results have appeared yet (a simulation study posted on 18 September 2026 notes that operational data from the optical link are not yet available), so treat the end date as provisional.[^lachmann2026]

### Back to the mountain

The mountain above Echo Lake, where Rossi and Hall took their van, was renamed Mount Blue Sky in September 2023,[^bluesky] and in the summer of 2025 its summit hosted an optical clock. The Relativistic Redshift Peak-to-Plains collaboration, led by NIST with the University of Colorado Boulder, links a ytterbium clock on the 4,300 m summit with another at NIST in Boulder, at 1,650 m, through 69 km of open air and 81 km of fibre. The summit clock should gain about 25 ns a day. In a test run in August 2025 the link held steady to a few parts in $10^{17}$; the aim is the $10^{-18}$ level, where the comparison pins down the height difference to about a centimetre.[^r2p2][^cuboulder] Status: the campaign was planned over three summers, and no final result had been published by September 2026.

### Nuclear clocks

Every clock so far counts the swinging of electrons in atoms. The next kind counts a nucleus. Thorium-229 has an excited nuclear state so low in energy that ultraviolet light at about 148 nm can reach it, and in 2024 three groups did it with lasers: a team from PTB in Germany and TU Wien in Austria in April, a UCLA team in July, and in September a JILA team that pinned the frequency at 2,020,407,384,335 kHz, give or take 2 kHz, and compared it directly with a strontium atomic clock.[^tiedau2024][^elwell2024][^zhang2024] A nucleus is small and shielded by its electrons, so it should be less disturbed by its surroundings, and far more nuclei fit in a crystal than atoms fit in the best atomic clocks.[^ooi2026]

In January 2026 the JILA group reported the frequency reproducible to 220 Hz over seven months in two differently doped calcium fluoride crystals, with a sweet spot near 196 K where temperature changes barely matter.[^ooi2026] In June 2026 members of the same PTB and TU Wien collaboration posted a preprint on a thorium clock running with a feedback loop, approaching one part in $10^{15}$ over a day, and used it to limit some models of ultralight dark matter, which would make the constants of nature wobble.[^toscani2026] Status: working prototypes, about a thousand times less stable than the best optical clocks at $10^{-18}$;[^takamoto2020] the June 2026 result was not yet peer-reviewed, and nobody can yet date a nuclear clock that beats the best atomic ones.

### A new second, and a clock for the Moon

The second is still defined by caesium. The best optical clocks now beat the caesium standard by a factor of up to a hundred.[^cgpm2026] The General Conference on Weights and Measures meets at Versailles on 13–15 October 2026, and its draft resolution on the future definition of the second asks for work towards a formal proposal at its next meeting, in 2030; which atom, ion or set of them to use is still open.[^cgpmdates][^cgpm2026]

::: note The second has relativistic fine print
The official rules already treat the SI second as a unit of proper time: what a caesium clock ticks off where it sits.[^cgpm2026] At the $10^{-18}$ level a clock must also know its height to about a centimetre to keep the same second as a clock in another country.
:::

The same meeting will vote on time for the Moon. The International Astronomical Union defined a Lunar Coordinate Time in 2024, and a draft resolution asks for a single international lunar reference time scale, traceable to UTC.[^cgpm2026] It is needed because a clock on the Moon, in weaker gravity, gains about 56 μs a day on one on Earth.[^ashbypatla2024] Status: to be voted on in October 2026; the draft does not name an organisation to run the time scale or a start date.

### The questions still open

Two questions remain open. One is whether the constants of nature are constant, and comparing different kinds of clock is among the most sensitive ways to check. The other is how clocks behave where quantum mechanics and gravity meet. The JILA group framed its millimetre measurement as a step towards clocks sensitive to the spread-out wavefunction of a quantum object in curved spacetime, a regime no experiment has reached.[^bothwell2022] There is no date for that one.
