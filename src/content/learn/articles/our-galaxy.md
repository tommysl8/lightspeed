In the most crowded part of the Milky Way, William Herschel once counted 588 stars in a single field of view. The field was 15 arcminutes across, half the width of the full Moon, and the stars kept coming: as the sky turned, he reckoned, 116,000 of them passed through it in a quarter of an hour.[^herschel1785] The telescope was his 20-foot reflector, with a mirror 18.7 inches (47.5 cm) across, and he was using it as a measuring rod. Where he counted more stars, he argued, the Milky Way had to reach further into space. Count in enough directions and you could draw its shape.

He did it with his sister. On a working night William stood high up on the telescope while Caroline Herschel sat indoors by a window with the star atlases, a system of strings telling her where he was pointing. She wrote down what he saw and did the calculations that turned his readings into positions.[^lhcaroline] On 3 February 1785 William read the result to the Royal Society in London: a flattened, branching slab of many millions of stars, drawn in cross-section, with a large star near the middle to mark the Sun.[^herschel1785]

The drawing was careful, honest about its limits, and wrong in the way that mattered most. The Sun is nowhere near the middle of the Galaxy. Astronomers needed more than 130 years to find that out, and another twelve to see why Herschel could not have known. The Milky Way is full of dust, and in visible light the dust closes off the view a few kiloparsecs away along the disc, whichever way you look.[^okw1958] Everything since has been a search for ways round it.

A parsec is 3.26 light-years, and a kiloparsec (kpc) is 1,000 of them, about 3,260 light-years. [How far are the stars?](#/learn/how-far-are-the-stars) explains where the unit comes from. Galaxies are big enough that astronomers measure them mostly in kiloparsecs.

## A river of stars

The Greeks argued about what the Milky Way was. Aristotle recorded that Anaxagoras and Democritus took it to be the light of stars, and then explained at length why they must be wrong. He treated it like a comet instead: a glow in the upper air, lit by the turning of the heavens.[^aristotle] His version lasted nearly two thousand years.

Galileo Galilei ended it in a paragraph. He turned a telescope that made things look about thirty times nearer on the band of light, and in March 1610 he published what he saw in *Sidereus Nuncius*, printed in Venice. "The Galaxy is nothing else but a mass of innumerable stars planted together in clusters," he wrote, adding with some satisfaction that the evidence of the eyes had settled centuries of philosophers' disputes.[^galileo1610] Other faint patches, such as Praesepe in Cancer, broke up into stars as well.

That left the question of why the stars made a band. Thomas Wright, a self-taught tutor and garden designer near Durham, offered an answer in 1750 in *An Original Theory or New Hypothesis of the Universe*. Suppose the stars fill a broad, thin layer and we sit inside it. Look along the layer and you see star behind star, piling up into a glowing strip; look out of it and you see only a few.[^lhwright] Wright stopped short of saying that the Milky Way itself was a disc.

Immanuel Kant took that step. He knew Wright's book only from a summary in a Hamburg journal of 1751, but it led him to see the stars as a system arranged about a common plane, as the planets are. In his *Universal Natural History and Theory of the Heavens* of 1755 he went further. The faint oval nebulous stars in the sky, he suggested, could be whole systems like ours, flattened and so far away that their light is feeble.[^kant1755] The book appeared without his name, and its publisher went bankrupt as it came out; his stock was sealed up and the book stayed almost unknown for years.[^kant1755] Whether Kant's nebulae really were other galaxies was settled only in the 1920s, and [Island universes](#/learn/island-universes) tells that story.

Herschel turned Wright's picture into a measurement. His method rested on two assumptions: that stars are spread fairly evenly through the Milky Way, and that his telescope could see all the way to its edge. If both held, the number of stars in a field told him how far the system reached in that direction, the way counting trees along a straight line tells you how deep a wood is. In his deepest direction he put the edge at 497 times the distance of Sirius.[^herschel1785] With Sirius at its modern distance of 2.64 parsecs, that is about 1,300 parsecs, or 4,300 light-years.[^hip2007] Other directions gave less, and the result was a ragged disc, much wider than thick, with the Sun near the centre.

Both assumptions were wrong. Stars are not spread evenly, and no telescope looking along the disc in visible light reaches the edge. Dust dims everything beyond a few thousand parsecs, so every direction in the plane looks about as deep as every other, and whoever does the counting finds themselves in the middle.

## The Sun slides off-centre

The same trap caught the best star counter of the early twentieth century. In 1922 the Dutch astronomer Jacobus Kapteyn published the finished version of Herschel's idea, built on photographic plates and statistics Herschel never had. His model was a flattened lens of about 47 billion stars. The surface where the density of stars fell to one hundredth of its value near the Sun reached 8,465 parsecs from the centre in the plane of the Milky Way, and 1,660 parsecs towards its poles. That makes a lens about 17 kpc (55,000 light-years) across and five times wider than thick, with the Sun some 650 parsecs from the middle.[^kapteyn1922] In the same paper, almost in passing, Kapteyn suggested that one day it might be possible to weigh the "dark matter" in the system by its gravity.[^kapteyn1922] He meant dim stars and dark clouds. The phrase would outlive his model by a century.

Four years earlier, a young astronomer at Mount Wilson in California had reached a very different answer by looking at something else. Harlow Shapley studied globular clusters, tight balls of up to millions of stars that mostly sit well away from the Milky Way's band. Using several yardsticks, starting with the pulsating stars inside them, he worked out distances to 69 clusters, from 6,500 to 67,000 parsecs.[^shapley1918] Then he plotted them in three dimensions.

The clusters were spread evenly above and below the plane of the Milky Way, but not around the Sun. They crowded towards one part of the sky, in Sagittarius, a few degrees from its borders with Scorpius and Ophiuchus. A system that big, Shapley reasoned, must be centred on the centre of the whole Galaxy, so the centre was nowhere near the Sun. He put it, provisionally, about 13,000 parsecs away in Sagittarius, and said the true figure was probably larger.[^shapley1918] That is 42,000 light-years, and it moved the Sun out towards the edge of an enormous disc. His manuscript is dated December 1917.

Shapley also noticed something odd. No globular cluster lay within 1,300 parsecs of the plane of the Milky Way: there was a gap right along the band, where stars are thickest. He listed possible causes, including clouds of light-absorbing matter strung along the Milky Way, and then set that one aside. He preferred the idea that clusters simply could not survive in the crowded plane.[^shapley1918] He was looking straight at the dust and chose not to believe it.

Both men were partly right. Kapteyn's counts were good, but his lens was only the patch of the disc whose light could reach us. Shapley had the right direction, but his distance was about 60% too big, because dust had dimmed his clusters and made them look further away than they were. [Island universes](#/learn/island-universes) tells how the same argument spilled over into a famous debate about the spiral nebulae.

## Dust

Robert Trumpler at Lick Observatory in California settled the question in 1930 with open clusters, the loose groups of young stars that lie in the plane of the Milky Way. He worked out the distances of 100 of them in two ways.[^trumpler1930] The first used brightness: find how luminous the stars are from their spectra, compare with how bright they look, and use the rule that light fades with the square of distance. The second used size: clusters of the same type ought to be about the same size, so one that looks half as wide should be twice as far away.

The two methods disagreed, and not at random. For nearby clusters the size method gave the larger distances; for distant ones it gave the smaller. Trumpler looked for mistakes and found none. Unless open clusters grew bigger the further they were from the Sun, the inverse-square rule was failing. Something between the stars was absorbing light, dimming their photographic brightness by about 0.7 magnitudes for every 1,000 parsecs, and reddening it too, the way haze reddens a setting Sun.[^trumpler1930]

The clues had been there. Trumpler pointed out that Kapteyn had measured about the right amount of reddening as early as 1909, and that Shapley's globular clusters, which did not look reddened, had "discouraged further work on the subject".[^trumpler1930] The catch, obvious in hindsight, is that the clusters Shapley could see at all are the ones lying well away from the dusty plane.

### How much light gets through

Astronomers measure brightness in magnitudes, where bigger numbers mean fainter. One rule is enough here: a difference of 5 magnitudes is a factor of exactly 100 in brightness, so 1 magnitude is a factor of about 2.5. Dimming by $A$ magnitudes multiplies the light that reaches you by $10^{-0.4A}$. [How far are the stars?](#/learn/how-far-are-the-stars) has the whole scale.

Trumpler's 0.7 magnitudes per kiloparsec means that each kiloparsec lets through $10^{-0.28}$, or 52%, of the light. The line of sight to the centre of the Galaxy is far worse than that average, because it runs through thick clouds: it is dimmed by about 30 magnitudes in visible light.[^genzel2010] Thirty magnitudes is six factors of 100, and $100^6 = 10^{12}$. Of every million million photons of yellow light that leave a star near the centre and head our way, one arrives.

The dimming falls steeply with wavelength. In the near infrared, at 2.2 micrometres, the same line of sight is dimmed by only about 3 magnitudes, a factor of 16.[^genzel2010] That one fact decided how the centre of the Galaxy would be explored: with radio dishes and infrared cameras.

## A disc that spins

A disc of stars held together by its own gravity cannot sit still; it has to turn, or it would fall in on itself. In the mid-1920s the Swedish astronomer Bertil Lindblad proposed that the Galaxy is made of several systems turning about a common axis at different speeds. That would explain an old puzzle: globular clusters and some fast stars seem to drift past the Sun at 200 to 300 km/s, all in roughly the same direction.[^oort1927]

Jan Oort at Leiden found the proof, in a paper dated 14 April 1927.[^oort1927] If the disc turned like a solid wheel, nearby stars would keep their distances from one another and show no pattern at all. If it turned like the Solar System, with the inner parts going round faster, nearby stars would show one: those on the inside track pulling ahead of the Sun, those on the outside falling behind. Seen from the Sun, that makes a pattern in the stars' motions that repeats twice around the sky. Oort found it, and the centre it pointed to lay in the same direction as Shapley's clusters.[^oort1927]

To turn the pattern into a distance, Oort had to assume a speed for the Sun. He took 272 km/s and got 5,900 parsecs, cut to 5,100 in a note added while the paper was at the printers. To hold the stars in orbit at that speed, he calculated, the middle of the Galaxy needed at least 80 billion times the mass of the Sun. Then he asked why nobody could see that much stuff, and answered that most of the Galaxy was probably hidden by obscuring "dark matter".[^oort1927] He meant dust.

### Weighing a galaxy with one speed

For something on a circular orbit, gravity supplies exactly the pull needed to keep bending its path. If the mass doing the pulling is spread in a roughly round way, it acts as if it were all at the centre, and the speed of the orbit is set by the mass inside it:

$$v^2 = \frac{GM}{r}$$

In words: the square of the orbital speed equals the gravitational constant times the mass inside the orbit, divided by the radius of the orbit. Turned round, $M = v^2 r / G$, it weighs whatever is inside an orbit from the speed of anything going round it. The Galaxy is a flattened disc rather than a ball, so this is an approximation, but a good one for a first estimate.

Modern measurements give the Sun a circular speed of 229 km/s, from the motions of more than 23,000 red giant stars, and a distance from the centre of 8.18 kpc.[^eilers2019][^gravity2019] In SI units, $v = 229{,}000$ m/s and $r = 8{,}180 \times 3.086 \times 10^{16}$ m $= 2.52 \times 10^{20}$ m. Then $M = (229{,}000)^2 \times 2.52 \times 10^{20} / (6.674 \times 10^{-11}) = 1.98 \times 10^{41}$ kg, which is $1.0 \times 10^{11}$ solar masses: about 100 billion Suns inside the Sun's orbit. Oort, with the wrong speed and the wrong radius, had the right size.

The same numbers give the length of the Sun's year round the Galaxy. The orbit is $2\pi r = 1.58 \times 10^{21}$ m long, and at 229 km/s that takes $6.9 \times 10^{15}$ seconds, about 220 million years. Since it formed 4.6 billion years ago the Sun has been round about 21 times. When it started its current lap, as the Nobel committee's background notes put it, dinosaurs were walking the Earth.[^nobelpop]

## Listening to hydrogen

In 1933 Karl Jansky, an engineer at Bell Laboratories tracking down the static that interfered with overseas radio links, reported a faint hiss at 20.5 MHz, a wavelength of about 14.5 metres.[^jansky1933][^nraojansky] He had built a rotating aerial that became known as his merry-go-round, and the hiss moved round the sky through the day. It came from the direction of Sagittarius, where Shapley had put the centre of the Galaxy. Radio waves pass through interstellar dust without hindrance.[^okw1958]

A hiss, though, carries no distance. What Oort wanted was a spectral line: radiation at one exact frequency from one kind of atom, whose Doppler shift would tell how fast its source was moving. In early 1944, with the Netherlands under German occupation, he suggested a problem to a young student from Utrecht, Hendrik van de Hulst. As van de Hulst remembered it, Oort told him that radio astronomy "can really become very important if there were at least one line in the radio spectrum".[^vw2006]

Van de Hulst found one in the simplest atom there is. A hydrogen atom is a proton and an electron, and both spin. The atom has slightly less energy when the two spins point in opposite directions than when they point the same way, and once in a long while an atom in the higher state flips to the lower one and gives out a photon at 1,420.4 MHz, a wavelength of 21.1 cm. For any one atom the wait is enormous, about 11 million years on average. But the Galaxy holds so much hydrogen that the combined glow is strong enough to detect.[^vw2006] Van de Hulst presented the idea at a colloquium in Leiden on 15 April 1944. The war held up publication until December 1945, and even then he judged detection possible but far from certain.[^vw2006]

The Dutch set out to find the line and had bad luck. They had a German Würzburg radar dish, 7.5 metres across, salvaged after the war and set up at the Kootwijk radio station, and on 10 March 1950 a fire destroyed their receiver.[^vw2006][^nraokootwijk]

At Harvard, meanwhile, a graduate student called Harold Ewen and his supervisor Edward Purcell had a $500 grant and built a horn antenna, which they fixed to the outside of the fourth floor of the Lyman Laboratory, with a waveguide running in through a window to the receiver. It funnelled rainwater into the lab and, in winter, drew snowballs from passing students.[^nraohorn] On 25 March 1951 it picked up the line.[^vw2006]

What Purcell did next is one of the more generous moments in the history of science. Van de Hulst and the Australian radio astronomer Frank Kerr both happened to be at Harvard. Purcell asked them to report the detection to their home institutes and to find out whether it could be confirmed. Van de Hulst had already written home about the frequency-switching trick in Ewen's receiver, and with it the rebuilt Dutch equipment found the line at Kootwijk on 11 May 1951; in Sydney, a team ran a crash programme and had a detection within six weeks.[^vw2006] The American and Dutch reports, with a confirming telegram from Australia, appeared side by side in *Nature* on 1 September 1951.[^nature1951]

### Turning speed into a map

The line lets astronomers measure the speed of hydrogen along the line of sight by how far its frequency has shifted:

$$f_{\text{seen}} = f_0 \left(1 - \frac{v}{c}\right)$$

In words: the frequency you receive is the frequency the atom sent out, $f_0$, reduced by the fraction $v/c$, where $v$ is the speed at which the gas is moving away from you and $c$ is the speed of light. If the gas is coming towards you, $v$ is negative and the frequency goes up. This simple form is accurate to about one part in a million for gas moving at a few hundred km/s.

Take a cloud moving away at 100 km/s. Then $v/c = 100 / 299{,}792 = 0.000334$, and $f_{\text{seen}} = 1{,}420.406 \times (1 - 0.000334) = 1{,}419.932$ MHz. The shift is 0.474 MHz, about one part in 3,000, easy for a radio receiver to measure. A cloud approaching at 50 km/s shows up at 1,420.643 MHz.

Speeds become distances through geometry. Point a dish along the plane of the Galaxy at, say, 30 degrees from the direction of the centre. The line of sight crosses rings of gas at many radii, all orbiting. The ring it just grazes, at its closest approach to the centre, is moving straight along the line of sight, so it shows the biggest shift of all, and simple trigonometry puts that ring at a radius of $R_0 \sin 30° = 0.5\,R_0$, about 4.1 kpc from the centre. Measure the biggest shift at every angle and you have the rotation speed at every radius inside the Sun's orbit. Once you know how speed depends on radius, every other shift in a spectrum can be turned into a distance, although inside the Sun's orbit each speed matches two places, one near and one far, and sorting them out took decades.

In 1958 Oort, Kerr and Gart Westerhout put the Dutch and Australian surveys together into a map of hydrogen across the whole disc.[^okw1958] It has two empty wedges, one pointing at the centre and one directly away. Along those lines the gas moves across our line of sight, not along it, so there is no shift to turn into a distance. Arms stood out, and the names given them stuck: the Perseus arm, the Sagittarius arm, and the Orion arm, near whose inner edge the Sun sits. The authors judged that the Galaxy would look much like the Andromeda galaxy or M81.[^okw1958]

## The far side

Doppler distances rest on a model of how the Galaxy rotates, and on the far side of the centre the model is least reliable. The cure is to measure distances directly by parallax, the method Friedrich Bessel used for the star 61 Cygni in 1838, told in [How far are the stars?](#/learn/how-far-are-the-stars), but now across the Galaxy. The targets are masers: dense knots of gas in star-forming clouds where water or methanol molecules amplify microwaves into bright, compact beacons. Radio dishes thousands of kilometres apart, combined into one instrument, can follow their positions over a year closely enough to see them trace tiny parallax ellipses.

In 2017 a team led by Alberto Sanna measured the parallax of a water maser beyond the centre of the Galaxy. The answer was 20.4 kpc, or 66,500 light-years, in the Scutum–Centaurus arm on the far side of the disc.[^sanna2017] The parallax behind that distance is 49 millionths of an arcsecond, the angle covered by a coin 2.3 cm across seen from a quarter of the way to the Moon. In 2019 Mark Reid and colleagues combined about 200 maser parallaxes into a map of the arms and a new set of basic numbers: a distance to the centre of 8.15 ± 0.15 kpc and a rotation speed at the Sun of 236 ± 7 km/s. Their arms, they wrote, strongly suggest a spiral with four major arms.[^reid2019]

## Something heavy in Sagittarius

On 13 and 15 February 1974, Bruce Balick and Robert Brown pointed the National Radio Astronomy Observatory's interferometer at Green Bank, West Virginia, at the radio source in Sagittarius. The instrument linked the Green Bank dishes with a 45-foot antenna 35 km away at Huntersville, and served as a test bed for the Very Large Array then under construction. It picked out a compact source less than an arcsecond across, sitting inside the clutter of hot gas at the centre. Balick and Brown argued that it was physically tied to the Galaxy's nucleus and, in effect, marked the centre of the Galaxy.[^goss2003][^balick1974]

They had reasons to look. In 1971 Donald Lynden-Bell and Martin Rees had argued that the violent cores of distant quasars might have a quiet cousin in our own galaxy, and they listed ways to test it. One was to use radio interferometers with very long baselines to "determine the size of any central black hole that there may be in our galaxy".[^goss2003] That test would take until 2022.

The source had no proper name for eight years. In 1982 Brown, thinking of it as the source that excited the surrounding clouds of glowing gas, borrowed the notation of his PhD in atomic physics, where an excited atom gets an asterisk: He\*, Fe\*. So, Sgr A\*.[^goss2003]

Seeing stars around it took the infrared, where 30 magnitudes of dust shrink to about 3.[^genzel2010] It also took beating the atmosphere, which smears stars into blobs. From the early 1990s two teams took this on. Reinhard Genzel's group at the Max Planck Institute for Extraterrestrial Physics started on ESO's New Technology Telescope in Chile and moved to the Very Large Telescope, whose four mirrors are each more than 8 metres across. Andrea Ghez's group at UCLA used the Keck telescopes on Mauna Kea, with mirrors almost 10 metres across made of 36 segments each, and began there in 1995.[^nobelpop][^do2019] With better and better adaptive optics, which use a thin, flexible mirror to undo the air's turbulence as it happens, they measured stars creeping across the sky within an arcsecond of Sgr A\*.

By 1996 and 1998 both teams had found that stars closer in move faster, with speeds of several hundred km/s within about 0.01 parsecs, just as planets closer to the Sun move faster. The central mass that implied was 2 to 3 million Suns.[^eckart1996][^ghez1998][^bhg2016] The star that made the case is called S2 by the German team and S0-2 by the American one. It goes round on a long, thin ellipse, and in 2002 Genzel's group followed it through its closest approach and published an orbit of 15.2 years; the modern value is 16.05.[^schodel2002][^gravity2020] Both teams have now watched it go all the way round.

### Weighing a black hole with Kepler

Kepler's third law, in the units that make the Earth's orbit come out right, weighs whatever sits at the focus of an orbit:

$$M = \frac{a^3}{P^2}$$

In words: the mass at the centre, in solar masses, equals the cube of the orbit's semi-major axis (its average distance) in astronomical units, divided by the square of the orbital period in years. For the Earth round the Sun, $1^3 / 1^2 = 1$. The mass of the orbiting star is left out, which is fine when it is tiny compared with what it orbits.

GRAVITY, which appears in the next section, measured the semi-major axis of S2's orbit on the sky as 0.125058 arcseconds and its period as 16.0455 years.[^gravity2020] At a distance of 8,178 parsecs, an angle of one arcsecond spans 8,178 au; that is what a parsec means. So $a = 0.125058 \times 8{,}178 = 1{,}023$ au, and $M = 1{,}023^3 / 16.0455^2 = 1.070 \times 10^9 / 257.5 = 4.16$ million solar masses. At its closest, S2 comes within $a(1 - e) = 119$ au of that mass, using the orbit's eccentricity $e = 0.8846$: about four times Neptune's distance from the Sun, or 16.6 light-hours.

The distance to the centre matters a lot here. The mass depends on $a^3$, and $a$ is proportional to the distance, so a 1% error in the distance becomes a 3% error in the mass. Put the centre at 8,277 parsecs instead and the same orbit gives 4.31 million. That is why the mass of Sgr A\* and the distance to the centre of the Galaxy are always measured together, from the same stars.

Four million solar masses inside a region the size of the Solar System, giving out almost no light, rules out a cluster of dim stars or dead ones. Any spread-out mass inside S2's orbit must be less than 3,000 solar masses, under 0.1% of the total.[^gravity2022] A supermassive black hole is the only explanation anyone has.[^nobel2020]

::: myth The Milky Way turns around its central black hole, which holds the Galaxy together.
Sgr A\* has about 4.3 million solar masses; the Galaxy inside the Sun's orbit has about 100 billion.[^gravity2022][^eilers2019] On its own, the black hole would pull the Sun round at 1.5 km/s, not 230. Its gravity dominates only within a couple of parsecs of the centre, and the Sun would orbit almost exactly as it does if the black hole were not there at all.
:::

## Einstein at the closest approach

S2's closest approach in May 2018 was a test booked 16 years in advance. At that point the star moves at about 7,700 km/s, 2.6% of the speed of light, deep in the black hole's gravity, just where Einstein's corrections to Newton should show.[^gravity2018][^gravity2022] To catch it, Genzel's group and its partners built GRAVITY, an instrument that combines light from the four big telescopes of the VLT so that, for measuring positions, they act like a single telescope 130 metres across. It can place a star relative to Sgr A\* to within a few tens of microarcseconds.[^esogravityplus][^esogravity]

Two effects should stretch the wavelength of S2's light at its closest approach, on top of the ordinary Doppler shift from its motion. A fast-moving clock ticks slowly, the effect told in [Time dilation is real](#/learn/time-dilation), so the star's light waves arrive stretched. And light climbing out of a deep gravitational well loses energy, which is the gravitational redshift. For speeds well below $c$ and gravity that is strong but not extreme, the two add:

$$z \approx \frac{GM}{rc^2} + \frac{v^2}{2c^2}$$

In words: the extra fractional stretch in wavelength, $z$, is the depth of the gravitational well at the star's distance $r$, measured in units of $c^2$, plus half the square of the star's speed as a fraction of the speed of light. Astronomers usually multiply $z$ by $c$ and quote it as the speed that would give the same Doppler shift.

Take $M = 4.3$ million solar masses. For the Sun, $GM/c^2$ is 1.477 km, so for Sgr A\* it is 6.35 million km. At the closest approach, $r = 119$ au $= 1.79 \times 10^{10}$ km, so $GM/(rc^2) = 6.35 \times 10^{6} / 1.79 \times 10^{10} = 3.55 \times 10^{-4}$, which times $c$ is 106 km/s. For the speed term, $v = 7{,}750$ km/s gives $v/c = 0.0259$ and $v^2/(2c^2) = 3.35 \times 10^{-4}$, or 100 km/s. The total is about 200 km/s. That is small next to the star's speed along our line of sight, which reached almost 3,800 km/s in March 2018, but it is exactly the size GRAVITY's spectra were built to see.[^do2019]

They saw it. In 2018 the GRAVITY team reported a redshift parameter of $0.90 \pm 0.09 \pm 0.15$, on a scale where 0 means Newton and 1 means Einstein; by 2019, with more data, it was $1.04 \pm 0.05$. Ghez's team at Keck, with their own measurements, found $0.88 \pm 0.17$.[^gravity2018][^gravity2019][^do2019] In 2020 GRAVITY reported the next effect: S2's orbit does not close. Each time round, the whole ellipse swings forward by 12 arcminutes, the same kind of creep that shifts Mercury's orbit, here in a single 16-year lap.[^gravity2020]

That year Genzel and Ghez shared half the Nobel Prize in Physics "for the discovery of a supermassive compact object at the centre of our galaxy". The other half went to Roger Penrose, for showing that black holes are a robust prediction of general relativity.[^nobel2020] The committee's wording was careful. The orbits prove a compact mass; that it is a black hole is the only known explanation.

### The distance to the centre

The same data measure the distance to the centre by pure geometry. The orbit's size on the sky, in arcseconds, and its size in kilometres, which follows from the speeds measured along our line of sight, must describe the same ellipse. In 2019 GRAVITY got $8{,}178 \pm 13 \pm 22$ parsecs, or 26,670 light-years.[^gravity2019] Then things got awkward. Keck's value, published the same year, was $7{,}946 \pm 50 \pm 32$ parsecs, a disagreement at about two standard deviations.[^do2019] And GRAVITY's own number moved: with a refined analysis and more stars, their 2022 value was $8{,}277 \pm 9$ parsecs, with a further systematic uncertainty of about 30.[^gravity2022] Each team quotes its distance to better than 1%, and the two answers differ by 4%.

## A shadow the size of a doughnut on the Moon

Lynden-Bell and Rees's test needed a radio telescope the size of the Earth. The Event Horizon Telescope is the nearest thing to one: a network of radio observatories, including ALMA in Chile, the IRAM 30-metre telescope in Spain and the South Pole Telescope, which record radio waves 1.3 mm long at the same moments so that the recordings can later be combined as if they came from one dish. Eight observatories watched Sgr A\* on five nights between 5 and 11 April 2017, and the best data came on 7 April.[^eht2022]

It took five years and more than 300 researchers from 80 institutes to turn the data into a picture, released at simultaneous press conferences on 12 May 2022.[^eso2208] The difficulty was the black hole's small size. Gas near M87\*, the giant black hole the EHT imaged first, takes days to weeks to go round it; around the much smaller Sgr A\* it takes minutes, so the source kept changing while it was being photographed.[^eso2208] The published image is an average: a ring of glowing gas $51.8 \pm 2.3$ microarcseconds across around a dark centre.[^eht2022] ESO compared its size on the sky to a doughnut on the surface of the Moon.[^eso2208]

### Predicting the size of the ring

Light passing close to a black hole is bent so strongly that some of it goes round the hole before escaping. For a black hole that does not spin, the dark region in the middle, the shadow, is $2\sqrt{27}$ times $GM/c^2$ across, about 10.4 times. Seen from a distance $D$, it covers an angle

$$\theta \approx \frac{2\sqrt{27}\,GM}{c^2 D}$$

In words: the shadow's angular size, in radians, is about ten times the black hole's natural length $GM/c^2$, divided by its distance from us. A spinning black hole's shadow is smaller, but by less than about 8%.[^eht2022]

For Sgr A\*, $GM/c^2 = 6.35$ million km from the stars' orbits, so the shadow is $10.39 \times 6.35 \times 10^6 = 6.6 \times 10^7$ km across, about 0.44 au. Dividing by $D = 8{,}178$ parsecs $= 2.52 \times 10^{17}$ km gives $\theta = 2.61 \times 10^{-10}$ radians. One radian is 206,265 arcseconds, so $\theta = 5.4 \times 10^{-5}$ arcseconds, or 54 microarcseconds. The EHT measured a shadow of $48.7 \pm 7.0$ microarcseconds inside its bright ring.[^eht2022] Stars orbiting 1,000 au out predicted the size of something less than half an astronomical unit across, and the radio picture agreed.

In 2024 the collaboration published the ring in polarised light. It shows strong, ordered magnetic fields spiralling near the edge of the black hole, much like those round M87\*.[^eht2024][^eso2406]

## A galaxy built from wreckage

Gaia, the European Space Agency's star-mapping satellite, measured positions, distances and motions for about two billion stars between July 2014 and January 2025.[^esagaia] [How far are the stars?](#/learn/how-far-are-the-stars) tells how. With motions for so many stars, astronomers could do something new: run the orbits backwards and sort the stars into families.

In 2018 two teams found the same family among the old stars of the halo around the Sun. Vasily Belokurov and colleagues, using Gaia's first release and the Sloan Digital Sky Survey, found that many of these stars move on long, thin orbits that plunge in and out of the Galaxy, and argued that they came from a single galaxy of more than ten billion solar masses that fell in between 8 and 11 billion years ago.[^belokurov2018] Amina Helmi's team, with Gaia's second release, showed that a large share of the halo stars near the Sun move on stretched orbits slightly backwards compared with the disc, and have a chemistry that does not match stars born in the Milky Way. They were debris from a galaxy that, when it fell in about 10 billion years ago, was slightly more massive than the Small Magellanic Cloud is today, with the young Milky Way about four times heavier.[^helmi2018] Thirteen of the Milky Way's globular clusters came in with it.[^helmi2018]

Helmi's team called it Gaia-Enceladus, after the Giant of Greek myth who was a child of Gaia, the Earth, and Uranus, the sky, and who was buried under Mount Etna, where he was blamed for earthquakes. Their paper lists the parallels with obvious enjoyment: a giant among the Milky Way's satellites, buried both in the Galaxy and in the Gaia data, and responsible for shaking things up.[^helmi2018] Astronomers now often call it Gaia-Enceladus/Sausage, keeping both teams' names.[^haberle2024] The shaking was real. The merger stirred up the young disc of the Milky Way and helped puff part of it into the thick disc of old stars that surrounds the thin disc today.[^helmi2018]

The Galaxy we see is the survivor of a violent youth. ESA's summary of what Gaia found puts it plainly: instead of forming alone, the Milky Way merged with another large galaxy around 10 billion years ago.[^esagaia]

::: see-it go:omega-centauri
Omega Centauri, 5.43 kpc away with about 10 million stars, looks like the biggest globular cluster in the Galaxy. Most astronomers think it is the stripped core of a swallowed galaxy, probably the heart of Gaia-Enceladus itself. In 2024 a team using 20 years of Hubble images found seven stars near its centre moving faster than the cluster's escape speed, held there by something unseen of at least 8,200 solar masses: the nearest massive black hole known.[^haberle2024]
:::

## The weight of what we cannot see

Go back to $v^2 = GM/r$. If most of the Galaxy's mass were in its stars, which crowd towards the middle, orbital speeds far out should fall off the way the planets' speeds fall off with distance from the Sun. Stars and cold gas together come to about 63 billion solar masses.[^bhg2016] Put all of that at the centre and the speed 25 kpc out would be about 100 km/s.

It isn't. Using more than 23,000 red giants, Anna-Christina Eilers and colleagues found that the rotation speed falls only gently, by about 1.7 km/s for every kiloparsec, from 229 km/s at the Sun to about 200 km/s at 25 kpc.[^eilers2019] Put 200 km/s and 25 kpc into the equation and the mass inside comes out at $2.3 \times 10^{11}$ solar masses, nearly four times the mass of all the stars and gas. The rest is dark matter: something that pulls but neither shines nor blocks light. The name is the one Kapteyn and Oort used, but the meaning has changed. They meant dim stars and dust; the modern dark matter is spread through a roughly round halo that reaches far beyond the disc, and nobody knows what it is made of. Near the Sun it is thin: about 0.3 GeV per cubic centimetre, the mass of a third of a proton in every sugar-cube-sized volume.[^eilers2019]

How much there is in total is honestly uncertain. From the motions of distant globular clusters, Laura Watkins and colleagues found a total of about 1.5 trillion solar masses, with an uncertainty of roughly half that.[^watkins2019] In 2023 a team using Gaia's third release reported that the rotation curve drops by about 30 km/s between 19.5 and 26.5 kpc, and that the whole Galaxy might weigh only about 200 billion solar masses.[^jiao2023] Those two estimates differ by a factor of seven. Part of the trouble is that the outer Milky Way is not in a steady state. The Large Magellanic Cloud may weigh 140 billion solar masses by itself, enough to set the outer halo moving,[^erkal2019] and equations like $v^2 = GM/r$ assume orbits that have settled down.

## What comes next

### Gaia Data Release 4

**Status:** scheduled for 2 December 2026, as of September 2026.[^esarelease] It is built from 66 months of observations and covers about 2.8 billion sources, with a high-quality table of about 2 billion. It will also include the individual measurements behind each star, about 400 terabytes in all, forty times the size of the last release.[^esadr4] For the Galaxy, longer time coverage means better motions for faint and distant stars, which is exactly what is needed to trace old mergers and weigh the halo. The final release, DR5, using all the mission's data, is due not before the end of 2030.[^esarelease]

### The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope

**Status:** launched on 30 August 2026 on a Falcon Heavy and now being commissioned; its 300-megapixel infrared camera was switched on on 15 September, and science is due to begin in early 2027.[^romanblog] Its Galactic Bulge Time Domain Survey will photograph six fields near the centre of the Galaxy, 1.7 square degrees in all, every 12 minutes during six observing seasons, watching hundreds of millions of stars in infrared light that passes through much of the dust. NASA expects it to find hundreds of stellar-mass black holes as well as more than a thousand planets.[^romangbtds]

### Closer to Sgr A\*

**Status:** in progress, dates uncertain. ESO is upgrading GRAVITY to GRAVITY+ in stages, with better adaptive optics and a laser guide star on each of the four big telescopes, so that it can work with fainter targets. ESO lists its first light as "to be announced".[^esogravityplus] The GRAVITY team expects it to push the instrument's limit beyond magnitude 20 in the infrared, more than 200 times fainter than S2, which may reveal stars on even smaller orbits.[^gravity2022] ESO's 39-metre Extremely Large Telescope is scheduled to make its first test observations in 2029, with science instruments following in December 2030.[^esoelt] Both are targets, not promises.

### Movies of the black hole

**Status:** continuing, with a space mission proposed. Since 2017 the EHT has added telescopes and doubled the amount of data it records, with the stated aim of eventually making movies of the gas as it moves round Sgr A\*.[^eht2022] The Black Hole Explorer (BHEX) would add a radio dish in space to the EHT and try to see the thin photon ring made by light that has circled the black hole; its team plans to propose it to NASA as a Small Explorer in 2026, for launch in 2031.[^bhex] As of September 2026 it is a proposal, not an approved mission.

### The Square Kilometre Array

**Status:** under construction. SKA-Low in Western Australia made its first image on 17 March 2025, using 1,024 of its planned 131,072 antennas.[^skaolow] SKA-Mid in South Africa will have 197 dishes, including the existing MeerKAT telescope, spread over 150 km.[^skamid] Science verification is due to start towards the end of the second stage of assembly; the observatory's timeline gives stages rather than firm dates.[^skatimeline] Our own galaxy and cosmic magnetism are both on the observatory's list of science goals, and SKA-Mid is designed for timing pulsars.[^skagoals][^skamid]

### Open questions

Some of the biggest questions about our galaxy are still open. The masers suggest four main spiral arms, but we see the far side through the whole disc and the map there is still thin.[^reid2019] The total mass is uncertain by a factor of several, and so is the timing of the Milky Way's future.

::: myth The Milky Way is certain to collide with the Andromeda galaxy in a few billion years.
With current measurements it is close to a coin toss. In 2025 Till Sawala and colleagues followed the uncertainties in the measurements through to the future and found about a 50% chance that the two galaxies do not merge in the next 10 billion years. The pull of the Large Magellanic Cloud makes a merger less likely; that of the Triangulum galaxy makes it more likely.[^sawala2025]
:::

::: see-it local-group
The Local Group from outside: the Milky Way, Andromeda, Triangulum and the Magellanic Clouds. The two big spirals dominate, but the smaller galaxies around them are heavy enough to change how the story ends.
:::
