On Saturday 3 June 1769, at Matavai Bay in Tahiti, three men sat at three telescopes and watched a black dot creep across the Sun. They could not have asked for better weather. "Not a Cloud was to be seen the whole day," James Cook wrote in his journal.[^cookjournal] The thermometer hanging by their clock, out in the sun with them, reached 119 °F, about 48 °C.[^greencook1771]

Cook and Charles Green, the astronomer the Royal Society had sent, used identical reflecting telescopes magnifying 140 times. The botanist Daniel Solander had a stronger one. Their job was to time the moments when Venus, crossing the face of the Sun, touched the Sun's edge. Afterwards they compared notes, and the notes did not agree. For the instant Venus's disc touched the Sun's edge from inside, just as it began to leave, each man wrote down two times: one when the thread of sunlight between the two edges broke, one when the edges themselves seemed to meet. Green's pair was 3 h 14 min 3 s and 3 h 14 min 51 s; Cook's was 3 h 14 min 13 s and 3 h 14 min 45 s.[^greencook1771] Two careful observers, a few steps from each other, in perfect weather, and the single instant they had come for was smeared across as much as 48 seconds. Fifty-three years earlier Edmond Halley had assured the Royal Society that such a moment could be timed to a single second.[^halley1716]

The *Endeavour* had spent more than seven months at sea to collect those seconds.[^pogge26] They were meant to deliver the one number astronomy still lacked: the distance from the Earth to the Sun. By 1769 astronomers could draw the layout of the Solar System with confidence, every orbit the right shape and the right size compared with every other. Nobody could say how big any of it was in miles. The map had no scale bar. Adding one took two thousand years of geometry, a century of expeditions and, in the end, a radio signal that came back from Venus a little under seven minutes after it left the Mojave Desert.

## The shadow at Syene

The first number in the chain was the size of the Earth, because for the next two thousand years the Earth was the ruler astronomers laid across the sky. Around 240 BCE Eratosthenes, the chief librarian at Alexandria, heard from travellers about a well at Syene, the modern Aswan. At noon on the summer solstice the Sun lit the whole bottom of the well, which meant it stood straight overhead. At Alexandria on the same day, the shadow of a stick showed the noon Sun about 7.2 degrees from overhead, one fiftieth of a full circle.[^aps2006] His book on the measurement is lost. The method survives in later writers, among them the astronomer Cleomedes.[^mactutorerat]

The idea needs nothing but parallel lines. The Sun is so far away that its rays reach both cities travelling in the same direction. At Syene a sunbeam points straight down towards the centre of the Earth; at Alexandria the upright stick does too. Those two lines meet at the Earth's centre, and because the sunbeam at Alexandria is parallel to the one at Syene, the angle between it and the stick equals the angle between the two lines at the centre. The road from Alexandria to Syene is therefore 7.2/360 of the way round the world.

$$C = \frac{360^\circ}{\theta} \times s$$

In words: the Earth's circumference is the distance between two places on the same north-south line, multiplied by the number of times the angle between them fits into a full circle.

Eratosthenes put Syene 5,000 stadia from Alexandria, so $C = (360 \div 7.2) \times 5{,}000 = 50 \times 5{,}000 = 250{,}000$ stadia.[^aps2006] How good that is depends on the length of his stadion, which historians still argue about. Suggestions run from about 152 m to 183 m, and one reading of Pliny gives 157.2 m.[^aps2006][^mactutorerat] At 157.2 m his answer is 39,300 km; at 183 m it is 45,750 km. The real distance round the Earth through the poles is 40,008 km. His raw inputs were good: Alexandria is 7.1 degrees of latitude north of Aswan, about 790 km.

Educated Greeks accepted a round Earth from the time of Pythagoras and Aristotle, and educated Europeans never gave it up. The belief that medieval people thought otherwise was invented in the nineteenth century, spread by the French scholar Antoine-Jean Letronne and by Washington Irving's 1828 life of Columbus.[^russell1997]

## Aristarchus and the half moon

Aristarchus of Samos, who watched the summer solstice of 281 or 280 BCE, tried the next step outwards.[^heath1913] His short book *On the Sizes and Distances of the Sun and Moon* is the oldest surviving attempt to measure how far away the Sun is, and it rests on one clean observation. When the Moon is exactly half lit, sunlight is striking it side-on. The Earth, the Moon and the Sun then make a right-angled triangle, with the right angle at the Moon. Measure the angle at the Earth between the Moon and the Sun and you know the shape of the whole triangle.

$$\frac{d_{\text{Sun}}}{d_{\text{Moon}}} = \frac{1}{\cos\alpha}$$

In words: at half moon, the Sun is further away than the Moon by a factor of one divided by the cosine of the angle between them in the sky.

Aristarchus said the angle fell short of a right angle by one thirtieth of a right angle, which makes it 87 degrees.[^heath1913] Since $\cos 87^\circ = 0.0523$ and $1 \div 0.0523 = 19.1$, the Sun is about 19 times further away than the Moon, and he proved it lay between 18 and 20 times.[^heath1913] The geometry was flawless. The measurement was hopeless. With the Moon 384,400 km away and the Sun 149.6 million km, the true angle is 89.85 degrees and the true ratio is 389. Near a right angle the cosine is tiny and changes fast, so small errors explode: 89 degrees gives a ratio of 57, and 89.5 degrees gives 115. Worse, the Moon is exactly half lit about 17 minutes before it reaches 90 degrees from the Sun, and nobody can judge the moment of half moon by eye to within hours.

Even a Sun only 19 times further off than the Moon had to be big. Aristarchus worked out that its diameter lay between about six and seven times the Earth's, which makes it some 300 times the Earth's volume.[^heath1913] Archimedes, in *The Sand-Reckoner*, reports that Aristarchus proposed that "the earth revolves about the sun in the circumference of a circle".[^heath1913] Nobody knows what led him there. The Italian astronomer Giovanni Schiaparelli suggested it was the Sun's size: it is odd to make the much larger body circle the smaller one.[^heath1913]

The ratio stuck, and so did a small Sun. Ptolemy, in the second century CE, put it about 1,200 Earth radii away, and Copernicus and Tycho Brahe were still using much the same figure fourteen centuries later.[^halley1716][^pogge26] That is about 7.7 million km, too small by a factor of 20.

## A map with no scale bar

Nicolaus Copernicus's *On the Revolutions* was printed in Nuremberg in 1543, and he held a copy of the finished book on his deathbed on 24 May that year.[^sep] Putting the Sun at the centre did something the Earth-centred system never could: it tied the sizes of the planets' orbits to one another.

Venus shows how. It never strays more than about 46 degrees from the Sun in our sky. With the Sun at the centre, that limit is the moment when our line of sight just grazes Venus's orbit, so the angle at Venus between the Sun and the Earth is a right angle and the ratio of the two orbits is the sine of the greatest angle: $\sin 46.3^\circ = 0.723$. Copernicus got 0.719 for Venus, 0.376 for Mercury, 1.52 for Mars, 5.22 for Jupiter and 9.17 for Saturn, all as multiples of the Earth-Sun distance. The modern figures are 0.723, 0.387, 1.52, 5.20 and 9.54.[^pogge14]

Johannes Kepler made the map exact. Grinding through Tycho Brahe's observations of Mars (nearly a thousand sheets of his arithmetic survive), he found that planets move on ellipses with the Sun at one focus, and that the line from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times. Both rules appeared in *Astronomia nova* in 1609.[^mactutorkepler] A third rule, linking the size of each orbit to the time it takes, came to him on 8 March 1618, was rejected after an unlucky calculation, and came back on 15 May. He wrote that "at first I believed I was dreaming", and published it in *Harmonices mundi* in 1619.[^mactutorkepler]

$$P^2 = a^3$$

In words: for anything orbiting the Sun, the orbital period in years, squared, equals the average distance from the Sun in astronomical units, cubed. One astronomical unit (au) is, near enough, the Earth's average distance from the Sun.

Mars takes 687.0 days to go round the Sun, which is 1.881 years.[^nssdc] Square that and you get 3.538. The cube root of 3.538 is 1.524, so Mars's average distance is 1.524 au. Jupiter takes 4,331 days, or 11.86 years; $11.86^2 = 140.6$, whose cube root is 5.20. NASA's distances of 228.0 and 778.5 million km, divided by the Earth's 149.6 million, give 1.524 and 5.20.[^nssdc] A clock and a calendar are enough to map the planets.

The inner Solar System from above, a year in about half a minute. Mercury laps the Sun just over four times while the Earth goes round once, and Mars manages about half a lap. Kepler's third law turns those periods straight into distances.
:::

What a clock can never give you is the size in kilometres. Every distance on Kepler's map is a multiple of the au, and the au itself was unknown. The consolation was leverage: measure any one distance in kilometres, the Earth to Mars on a particular night say, and every other distance follows. Kepler argued that the traditional figure was far too small and put the Sun nearly 3,500 Earth radii away, about 22 million km.[^halley1716] He was still short by a factor of about seven.

## Paris and Cayenne, 1672

The obvious target was Mars at its closest, and the tool was parallax. Hold a finger at arm's length and close each eye in turn: the finger jumps against the background, and the further away it is, the smaller the jump. For Mars the two eyes had to be cities thousands of kilometres apart, and the jump was small enough to hide inside the planet's own disc.

On 8 February 1672 Jean Richer sailed from La Rochelle for Cayenne, in French Guiana, five degrees north of the equator. Through August, September and October he timed Mars crossing his meridian and measured its height against nearby stars, while Giovanni Domenico Cassini did the same at the new Paris Observatory.[^mactutorricher] Mars came closest on 5 September, 57.0 million km away.[^horizons]

Angles this small are easiest in radians. Wrap a length equal to a circle's radius around its edge, and the angle it covers at the centre is one radian. A full circle is $2\pi$ radians, so one radian is 57.3 degrees, or 206,265 arcseconds (an arcsecond is 1/3,600 of a degree). For small angles the curved arc and the straight line across it are practically the same length, so a length divided by its distance gives the angle it covers, in radians. Turn that round and you have the small-angle rule:

$$d = \frac{b}{\theta}$$

In words: the distance to an object equals the length of your baseline, measured across the line of sight, divided by the angle in radians through which the object shifts as you move from one end of the baseline to the other.

Measured across the line of sight to Mars on 5 September 1672, Paris and Cayenne were about 3,890 km apart. Mars should have shifted by $3{,}890 \div 57{,}000{,}000 = 6.8 \times 10^{-5}$ radians, which is $6.8 \times 10^{-5} \times 206{,}265 = 14$ arcseconds: a little over half the width of Mars's disc that night, or a 1 cm coin seen from 146 m. Now run the rule forwards. A measured shift of 14.1 arcseconds is $14.1 \div 206{,}265 = 6.84 \times 10^{-5}$ radians, so $d = 3{,}890\ \text{km} \div (6.84 \times 10^{-5}) = 56.9$ million km. Kepler's map says Mars was 0.381 au away that night, so one au is $56.9 \div 0.381 = 149$ million km.

Cassini's answer was 140 million km, 7% short, and the first measurement of the Sun's distance to land in the right neighbourhood.[^mactutorricher][^brumfiel2012] It corresponds to the Earth's radius, seen from the Sun, spanning 9.4 arcseconds. Astronomers called that angle the solar parallax and chased it for the next three centuries; today it is 8.794 arcseconds.

Richer brought home a second result that nobody had asked for. His seconds pendulum, set up in Paris, ran slow in Cayenne, and he had to shorten it by a line and a quarter, a little under 3 mm, to keep time. Newton later described it as a clock losing 2 minutes 28 seconds a day, and the result supported his argument that the Earth is flattened at the poles.[^mactutorricher] Richer's assistant, Meurisse, did not come home. He died in Cayenne in 1673.[^mactutorricher]

## Halley's long bet

Edmond Halley was 20 when he sailed for the island of St Helena in the South Atlantic in November 1676 to chart the southern stars. On 7 November 1677 he watched Mercury cross the face of the Sun.[^mactutorhalley] Through a 24-foot telescope he found he could catch the instants when Mercury's disc touched the Sun's edge from the inside almost perfectly.[^halley1716] Mercury was too far from the Earth to be much use for measuring the Sun's distance. Venus, which comes much closer, would be. At a transit Venus is only about 0.28 au from us, so by the small-angle rule the same baseline makes an angle $1 \div 0.28 = 3.6$ times bigger for Venus than for the Sun: its parallax, as Halley put it, is almost four times the Sun's.[^halley1716]

Only two people had ever seen Venus cross the Sun. Jeremiah Horrocks, a young astronomer living in the Lancashire village of Much Hoole, corrected Kepler's tables and predicted the transit of 4 December 1639 (24 November by England's old calendar). He caught it at about a quarter past three in the afternoon, when the clouds cleared. His friend William Crabtree saw it from Broughton, near Manchester.[^mactutorhorrocks] From his observations Horrocks estimated that the Sun was at least 15,000 Earth radii away, about 96 million km. He died in January 1641, aged 22.[^mactutorhorrocks]

Halley's plan, published in 1716, turned an angle nobody could measure into a time that a good clock could. Venus crosses the Sun's face at about 4 arcminutes an hour. Because of parallax, observers far apart on the Earth see it cross along slightly different lines, so their transits last for different lengths of time, and the difference depends on the Sun's distance. Halley reckoned that observers at Hudson's Bay and in India should find their transits differing by about 17 minutes. Nobody would have to measure a tiny angle, only start and stop a clock.[^halley1716]

The next transits would come in 1761 and 1769, when Halley would have been 104. He died in 1742.[^mactutorhalley] He asked for observers in many places, in case of cloud, and pressed the point: "again and again, I recommend it to the curious".[^halley1716] His details were wrong. He did not know how Venus's orbit had shifted since 1639, and in one step he added two angles that should have been subtracted, so his predicted tracks across the Sun were off.[^halley1716] The method was right.

## Venus, 1761 and 1769

The first transit, on 6 June 1761, fell in the middle of the Seven Years' War between Britain and France.[^pogge26] Charles Mason and Jeremiah Dixon sailed for Bencoolen in Sumatra aboard HMS *Seahorse*. Hours out of Portsmouth a French frigate attacked, killing 11 of the crew and wounding 37. By the time the ship was repaired and back at sea they could get no further than the Cape of Good Hope, so they observed from there, well enough that two years later they were hired to survey the border between Pennsylvania and Maryland.[^pogge26][^lindahallmason] Jean-Baptiste Chappe d'Auteroche crossed Russia by sledge to Tobolsk in Siberia, arrived with six days to spare and got good timings.[^pogge26]

Guillaume Le Gentil had the worst luck of anyone. He left France in March 1760 for the French trading post of Pondicherry in south-east India and learned on the way that the British had taken it. On transit day he was on a ship in the Indian Ocean, where a rolling deck made timing useless. He decided to stay for the next transit, eight years later. He reached Pondicherry in March 1768, built an observatory and enjoyed weeks of clear mornings. Early on 4 June 1769 a squall blew up and hid the Sun for exactly as long as his observation would have lasted. Venus left the Sun at seven o'clock; the Sun broke through at half past. Le Gentil wrote that he had sailed nearly ten thousand leagues to be the spectator of "un nuage fatal", a fatal cloud.[^danino2016] He crossed the Pyrenees back into France on 8 October 1771, eleven years, six months and thirteen days after he had left.[^danino2016]

::: myth Le Gentil came home to find himself declared dead and his wife remarried.
His heirs had decided he was dead and were preparing to share out his property, and the Académie des sciences had moved him to its list of veterans. There was no wife to lose: he married after he got back.[^danino2016][^pogge26]
:::

For 1769 the Jesuit astronomer Maximilian Hell went to Vardø in Arctic Norway, and Chappe went to San José del Cabo at the tip of Baja California, where he timed the transit well and died in an epidemic soon afterwards.[^pogge26] Cook, as we saw, had flawless weather at Tahiti. Many observers fought the problem Cook described. As the edge of Venus came away from the edge of the Sun, a dark neck seemed to stretch between them, the "black drop", and hid the true moment of contact. Instead of the 2 seconds Halley had hoped for, timings came out uncertain by about 10.[^pogge26]

::: myth The black drop was caused by the atmosphere of Venus.
Venus does have a thick atmosphere, which Mikhail Lomonosov inferred from a thin arc of light around the planet during the 1761 transit.[^marov2004] But a black drop also appears when airless Mercury crosses the Sun, even in images taken by a telescope in space. It comes from the slight blurring every telescope adds to an image, combined with the way the Sun's disc darkens towards its edge. Turbulent air on Earth makes it worse.[^schneider2004]
:::

In 1771 the French astronomer Jérôme Lalande combined the two transits and put the Sun 153 million km away, give or take a million.[^pogge26] That was 2% too far, and the best figure yet. When Simon Newcomb reworked the same eighteenth-century observations more than a century later, he got 149.7 million km, give or take 0.9 million.[^pogge26]

The next pair of transits, in 2004 and 2012, produced a coda. In 2004 the European Southern Observatory invited the public to repeat Halley's experiment. It collected 4,550 contact timings from 1,510 observers and got 149,608,708 km, give or take 11,835 km: 10,838 km, or 0.007%, from the true value.[^eso2004] Amateurs with accurate clocks, digital cameras and precisely known positions had beaten every expedition of the eighteenth century.

## Photographs, Eros and a stubborn gap

The transits of 9 December 1874 and 6 December 1882 came in the age of steam, telegraphs and photography.[^nasaeclipse] Most countries with scientific ambitions planned to observe them.[^dick2004] The United States Congress gave its Transit of Venus Commission 177,000 dollars for 1874 and 78,000 dollars for 1882, and each time eight American parties went out. They brought back 350 photographic plates in 1874 and 1,380 measurable plates in 1882. In 1894 William Harkness turned all this, with other data, into a solar parallax of 8.809 arcseconds, a distance of 92,797,000 miles or 149.34 million km, 0.17% short.[^dick2004]

It hardly counted. An international conference in Paris in 1896 adopted Simon Newcomb's system of astronomical constants, and in fixing the solar parallax Newcomb gave all the photographic transit observations together a weight of 2, against 40 for Pulkovo Observatory's result from the aberration of starlight.[^dick2004] Aberration is the slight forward tilt of starlight caused by the Earth's motion. If you know the speed of light, it gives you the Earth's orbital speed and so the size of its orbit; [Light takes time](#/learn/light-takes-time) tells that story. By 1896 the stopwatch had lost.

In 1898 the Berlin astronomer Gustav Witt found a small asteroid, 433 Eros, whose orbit brings it close to the Earth every so often.[^butrica1996] It looks like a point of light rather than a disc, so its position can be measured sharply. At its close pass of 1930 and 1931, observatories around the world took nearly 3,000 photographs, and from them Harold Spencer Jones, the Astronomer Royal, derived 149,675,000 km, give or take 17,000. Eugene Rabe of the Cincinnati Observatory tried another route. He followed how the pull of the Earth, Mars, Venus and Mercury bent the orbit of Eros between 1926 and 1945, and got 149,530,000 km, give or take 10,000.[^butrica1996]

Both men were careful, and each claimed an uncertainty of 10,000 to 17,000 km. They disagreed by 145,000 km. At least one of them was wrong by far more than he thought, and there was no way to tell which.

## An echo from Venus

Radar settles an argument like that by timing instead of looking. Send a radio pulse to Venus, time the echo, and the distance in kilometres follows with no angles at all.

$$d = \frac{c\,t}{2}$$

In words: the distance is the speed of light multiplied by the time the signal takes for the round trip, halved, because the signal went there and back.

On 11 April 1961 Venus was 0.2837 au from the Earth, a number that comes from the map alone.[^horizons] A radar pulse sent that day would have taken 283.1 seconds to come back. So $d = 299{,}792\ \text{km/s} \times 283.1\ \text{s} \div 2 = 42.44$ million km, and dividing by 0.2837 gives one au as 149.6 million km. Timing the echo to a thousandth of a second fixes the distance to 150 km, one part in a million.

The catch is that the echo is fantastically weak. On 10 and 12 February 1958 the Millstone Hill radar of MIT's Lincoln Laboratory fired pulses at Venus, then about 45 million km away, and a computer added up the faint returns. Two of the four usable runs showed a peak.[^butrica1996] The team published in *Science* on 20 March 1959, the story made the front page of the *New York Times*, and President Eisenhower sent a telegram of congratulations. The peaks implied an au of 149,467,000 km.[^butrica1996][^price1959] Later that year Jodrell Bank in England reported a confirmation, but to dig its signal out of the noise it had assumed the distance Lincoln had published. When Lincoln tried again in 1959 with nearly twice the transmitter power, it found nothing.[^butrica1996]

At the Jet Propulsion Laboratory, a Caltech graduate student named Richard Goldstein wanted to write his thesis on a radar attempt from JPL's Goldstone station in the Mojave Desert. His adviser thought it could not be done and agreed on one condition: "No echo, no thesis."[^butrica1996] On 10 March 1961 two 26-metre dishes at Goldstone, one transmitting 13 kW and the other listening through a maser amplifier, were pointed at Venus. The pen on Goldstein's recorder moved off zero and stayed there. To rule out a leak from their own transmitter, the team let the transmitting dish drift off Venus. About six and a half minutes later, roughly the time a round trip to Venus took that day, the pen fell back to zero. They did it again and got the same result.[^butrica1996]

Lincoln Laboratory, Jodrell Bank and Soviet radar astronomers all had echoes that year, and once their data were fully analysed they agreed with JPL, not with 1958.[^butrica1996] The final 1961 values were 149,598,845 km, give or take 250, from JPL, and 149,597,850 km, give or take 400, from Lincoln Laboratory, whose team conceded in print that its 1958 results were probably in error.[^muhleman1962][^pettengill1962] The 1958 peaks had been noise. The two Eros results had missed on either side of the truth, by 77,000 and 68,000 km.

The number mattered at once. In 1962 JPL steered Mariner 2 to Venus using the radar value; had it used Rabe's figure from Eros, the spacecraft would have passed Venus without collecting useful data.[^butrica1996] In 1964 the International Astronomical Union, meeting in Hamburg, adopted 149,600,000 km.[^butrica1996]

## Fixing the unit

Radar echoes from planets were soon joined by radio ranging to spacecraft, which is better still because a spacecraft answers back loudly. JPL's DE440 ephemeris, published in 2021, fits the orbits of the planets to decades of such data. Ranges to the MESSENGER spacecraft at Mercury, and to NASA's Mars orbiters, match the model with a scatter of about 0.7 m.[^park2021]

By then the unit itself had become the problem. From the nineteenth century until 2012 (officially from 1938) the au was not defined as a length at all. It was defined through the Sun's gravity using Carl Friedrich Gauss's constant, and amounted to the radius of the circle in which a body of negligible mass would orbit the Sun in 365.2568983 days.[^iau2012][^brumfiel2012] Measured in metres, a unit defined like that can drift, because the Sun is slowly losing mass, and once general relativity was taken into account it even came out slightly different in the reference frames of different planets.[^brumfiel2012] In 2004 G. A. Krasinsky and V. A. Brumberg reported from three decades of radar and spacecraft ranging that the au seemed to be growing by about 15 m per century, and could find no satisfying explanation.[^krasinsky2004]

In August 2012 the IAU, meeting in Beijing, voted unanimously to cut the knot.[^brumfiel2012] The au is now a fixed length, exactly 149,597,870,700 m, chosen to match the best measurement of the day. Gauss's constant was deleted, and the strength of the Sun's gravity, the product $GM_\odot$ of the gravitational constant and the Sun's mass, is now measured in SI units and allowed to change.[^iau2012]

It does change. Seven years of radio ranging to MESSENGER showed $GM_\odot$ falling by $6.1 \times 10^{-14}$ of itself each year, give or take about a quarter of that, which fits the mass the Sun sheds as sunlight and solar wind.[^genova2018] As the Sun's grip weakens, orbits widen in the same proportion. At the MESSENGER rate the Earth's grows by about 0.9 cm a year; one theoretical estimate, which assumes a somewhat faster loss of mass, puts it at 1.4 m per century, or 1.4 cm a year.[^genova2018][^noerdlinger2008] The au stays put.

::: myth The astronomical unit is the average distance from the Earth to the Sun.
Since 2012 it has been a defined length, exactly 149,597,870,700 m, which the IAU describes as approximating the Sun-Earth distance.[^iau2012][^iauunits] The Earth's real distance swings between 147.1 and 152.1 million km every year, and its orbit is slowly widening while the au stays fixed.[^nssdc][^noerdlinger2008]
:::

The au also sets the next rung of the ladder: a parsec is the distance at which one au would span one arcsecond.[^iauunits] [How far are the stars?](#/learn/how-far-are-the-stars) picks up from there, and [Clockwork and chaos](#/learn/clockwork-and-chaos) follows the mathematics that turns all this ranging into predictions.

Stand the 1 m Sun in a park. The Earth is a peppercorn 108 m away, the length of a football pitch, with a 2.5 mm Moon 28 cm from it. Mars is a smaller peppercorn 56 m further out. Jupiter, the biggest planet, is a 10 cm grapefruit more than half a kilometre away, and Neptune is a 3.6 cm ball a 40-minute walk from the Sun. Voyager 1, launched in 1977 and now further from the Sun than any other spacecraft, is 18.5 km out.[^lightday][^horizons] Proxima Centauri, the nearest other star, would be about 29,000 km away, most of the way round the real Earth. Sweden has built a model at 1 to 20 million, about 70 times bigger than this one: the Sun is the Globe arena in Stockholm, and Pluto sits in Delsbo, 300 km away.[^sss]

Take the main asteroid belt to run from about 2.1 to 3.3 au, and in the 1 m model it is a ring roughly 225 to 355 m from the Sun. NASA estimates it holds between 1.1 and 1.9 million asteroids larger than 1 km across.[^nasaasteroids] At this scale a 1 km asteroid is less than a thousandth of a millimetre wide, and spread evenly through the belt the specks are typically a couple of metres apart. In real space that is 3 to 4 million km, about ten times the distance to the Moon. Pioneer 10, the first spacecraft to fly through the belt, entered it on 15 July 1972 and came out the other side on its way to Jupiter.[^pioneer10]

::: see-it go:jupiter
The camera glides to Jupiter, 5.2 au out: in the 1 m model, a 10 cm ball more than half a kilometre from the Sun. It has about two and a half times the mass of all the other planets put together.
:::

Where the model should stop depends on what you count: the planets, the reach of the Sun's wind, or the reach of its gravity. [The edges of the Solar System](#/learn/edges-of-the-solar-system) goes looking for the boundary.

## What comes next

### The next transits of Venus

**Status:** predicted, and as certain as predictions get. The next transit runs from 23:58 UT on 10 December 2117 to 05:38 UT on 11 December, and the one after that from 13:15 to 18:48 UT on 8 December 2125.[^nasaeclipse] Working from where the Sun is overhead at mid-transit, the 2117 transit favours East Asia, Australia and the western Pacific, and the 2125 transit the Americas. Nobody will need them to measure the au, but they will be the first chance to see Venus cross the Sun since 2012.

::: see-it date:2117-12-11
The simulation date jumps to 11 December 2117. Find Venus: it sits almost exactly on the line from the Earth to the Sun, and from the Earth's day side it is a black dot crossing the Sun's face.
:::

### BepiColombo arrives at Mercury

**Status:** in its arrival phase. The European and Japanese mission, launched in 2018, released its transfer module on 3 September 2026. It is due to enter orbit around Mercury on 21 November 2026, split into its two orbiters on 9 to 10 December, and begin science in April 2027.[^esabepi] Its radio science experiment, MORE, carries a ranging system tested to 1 to 2 cm and measures speed to 3 thousandths of a millimetre per second; among its aims are sharper tests of general relativity.[^iess2021] For comparison, ranges to MESSENGER, the mission that caught $GM_\odot$ shrinking, scatter by about 0.7 m.[^park2021][^genova2018]

### A sharper map

**Status:** continuous. DE440, released in 2020, is JPL's most recent documented general-purpose ephemeris, and it covers the years 1550 to 2650.[^park2021] Files for a successor, DE442, appeared on JPL's server on 15 July 2026; at the time of writing no paper describing it had been published, so what changed has not been documented.[^jplftp]

### Planetary radar after Arecibo

**Status:** thin. Arecibo's 305 m dish in Puerto Rico was destroyed on 1 December 2020, when its 900-ton instrument platform fell into it after cables failed in August and November.[^guardian2020] By 2024 the Green Bank Observatory could describe NASA's Goldstone Solar System Radar as the only system in the world concentrating on this work.[^gbo2024] JPL's radar schedule shows Goldstone's 70 m transmitting dish, DSS-14, out of service for modernisation from 1 March 2026 to 1 May 2028, and a string of 2026 asteroid observations cancelled.[^jplradar] Pilot tests of ngRADAR, a proposed radar for the 100 m Green Bank Telescope, made what its team called the highest-resolution images of the Moon ever taken from the ground, using a transmitter weaker than a kitchen microwave.[^gbo2023][^gbo2024] No construction date for a full-power version has been announced.
