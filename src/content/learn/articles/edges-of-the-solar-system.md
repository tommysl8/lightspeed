On the afternoon of Tuesday 18 February 1930, in a small room at Lowell Observatory in Flagstaff, Arizona, Clyde Tombaugh sat at a Zeiss blink comparator with two glass photographs of the sky around the star Delta Geminorum. Each plate was 14 by 17 inches; one had been exposed on 23 January, the other on 29 January.[^tombaugh1946][^lowell] The machine flicked his view from one to the other and back. Stars stayed put. Anything that had moved in those six days would jump.

A quarter of the way through the pair, two faint specks about 3 mm apart jumped. "I suddenly came upon the images of Pluto!" he wrote later.[^tombaugh1946] Asteroids in that part of the sky slid about 7 mm a day on his plates. This crept half a millimetre, about right for something far beyond Neptune.[^tombaugh1946]

Tombaugh was 24. He had grown up on a farm near Burdett, Kansas, ground his own telescope mirrors and posted drawings of Mars and Jupiter to the observatory, whose director, V. M. Slipher, hired him in January 1929.[^nmsmh][^tombaugh1946] By that afternoon he had checked about two million stars. When he finally stopped, he had blinked through 90 million star images covering three-quarters of the sky.[^tombaugh1946]

The dot was Pluto, and for 76 years it was the ninth planet. It was really the first sighting of something nobody expected: a belt of icy bodies beyond Neptune, stranger objects beyond that, a bubble blown by the Sun's wind, and past all of it a cloud of comets that nobody has ever seen directly. Where the Solar System ends depends on which of those you count.

## Planet X, found and shrinking

Tombaugh was hunting for someone else's planet. Percival Lowell, whose name the observatory bears, had read small unexplained wobbles in the motion of Uranus as the pull of an unseen world, and in 1915 he gave "Planet X" seven Earth masses and an average distance of 43 au.[^tombaugh1946] (One au is the Earth's average distance from the Sun, 149.6 million km.) Lowell died in 1916.[^tombaugh1946] Tombaugh's dot lay within 6 degrees of where he had pointed,[^tombaugh1946] which, as [Clockwork and chaos](#/learn/clockwork-and-chaos) explains, was mostly luck.

The observatory telegraphed Harvard with the news on 13 March 1930.[^tombaugh1946] The next morning in Oxford, Falconer Madan read it out at breakfast, and his eleven-year-old granddaughter, Venetia Burney, suggested the name of the Roman god of the underworld. He passed it on, and the observatory took it.[^nasaplutofacts]

Nobody could weigh it. Through the observatory's 24-inch refractor Pluto showed no disc, only a faint point of light,[^tombaugh1946] and with nothing orbiting it the mass could only be guessed. Then, on 22 June 1978, James Christy of the US Naval Observatory was measuring that spring's photographs of Pluto from the 1.55 m telescope at Flagstaff and noticed the images were not round. On 13 and 20 April each had a faint bump to the south; on 12 May the bump was on the north. The stars on the same plates were round, so the telescope had not wobbled, and plates from 1965 and 1970 showed the bump too.[^christy1978] Pluto had a moon, blurred into it by the air. It was named Charon, after the ferryman of the dead, and also for Christy's wife Charlene.[^nasaplutofacts]

### How Charon weighed Pluto

A moon's orbit is a set of scales. Newton showed that the time two bodies take to circle each other depends only on how far apart they are and on their combined mass.

$$M = \frac{4\pi^2 a^3}{G\,P^2}$$

In words: the combined mass of two bodies in orbit around each other equals $4\pi^2$ times the distance between them cubed, divided by Newton's gravitational constant $G$ times the orbital period squared.

Charon is about 19,600 km from Pluto and goes round every 6.387 days.[^nasaplutofacts][^christy1978] In SI units $a = 1.96 \times 10^{7}$ m and $P = 551{,}800$ s. Then $a^3 = 7.53 \times 10^{21}$, and multiplying by $4\pi^2 = 39.5$ gives $2.97 \times 10^{23}$. Below the line, $G P^2 = 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times 3.05 \times 10^{11} = 20.3$. Dividing, $M = 1.46 \times 10^{22}$ kg. The Earth is $5.97 \times 10^{24}$ kg, so Pluto and Charon together come to 0.0024 Earth masses. Christy, with a blurrier separation of about 17,000 km, got 0.0017.[^christy1978]

Lowell's Planet X needed 7 Earth masses. Pluto alone has 0.0022, about a sixth of the Moon's mass.[^nssdcpluto][^nssdcmoon] It could never have pushed Uranus around. Planet X did not exist, and what Tombaugh found in its place was small, icy and, it turned out, not alone.

## The belt nobody predicted

Kenneth Edgeworth wrote about objects beyond Pluto in 1943 and 1949, in barely a paragraph, and a 1951 paper by Gerard Kuiper gave the belt its name.[^jewittkuiper][^edgeworth1949]

::: myth Gerard Kuiper predicted the Kuiper belt.
Kuiper assumed that Pluto was massive and had long ago flung the region's small bodies out to the distant comet cloud, so his paper, read at face value, predicts an empty zone. David Jewitt, one of the belt's discoverers, puts it bluntly: Kuiper "anti-predicted" what we now call the Kuiper belt. Fred Leonard had mentioned the possibility of such objects as early as 1930.[^jewittkuiper]
:::

The case that mattered came from comets. Most comets that return within 200 years orbit the same way round as the planets and close to their plane: of the 121 known in 1988, only four went backwards.[^dqt1988] In 1980 Julio Fernández suggested that they came from a belt just beyond Neptune.[^fernandez1980] In 1988 Martin Duncan, Thomas Quinn and Scott Tremaine checked with computer simulations. Comets fed in from a spherical swarm keep orbits tilted every which way, so only a flat source can make flat orbits. They called it the "Kuiper belt", and the name stuck.[^dqt1988]

Two people were already looking. In 1986 David Jewitt recruited Jane Luu to search beyond Neptune, and in 1987 they gave up photographic plates for the new electronic CCD cameras, far more sensitive but covering only tiny patches of sky.[^kavli2012] On 30 August 1992, at the University of Hawaii's 2.2 m telescope on Mauna Kea, a red dot of magnitude 22.8 crept 3 arcseconds an hour against the stars. The circular announcing it put it 37 to 59 au away and, if it was as dark as a comet and circled the Sun at 41 au, about 200 km across.[^iauc5611][^jewitt1993] It was 40.9 au from the Sun.[^horizons] 1992 QB1 was the first of many. By early 2026 about 5,000 objects beyond Neptune had been found, and the new Rubin Observatory then added 380 in six weeks of test observations.[^rubin2608]

## Reading a moving dot

Two simple ideas turn a moving dot into a distance and a size.

### How far away?

Look directly away from the Sun, at opposition, as Tombaugh did.[^tombaugh1946] The Earth moves at 29.8 km/s, overtaking anything further out, so a distant object seems to slide backwards against the stars, like a far-off tree past a car window. The further away it is, the slower it slides. In an hour the Earth covers 107,000 km, and the small-angle rule from [How big is the Solar System?](#/learn/how-big-is-the-solar-system) turns that into a rule of thumb, the one Mike Brown's team used to size up Sedna the moment they saw it.[^brown2004]

$$R \approx \frac{150}{\omega}$$

In words: seen at opposition, an object's distance from the Sun in au is roughly 150 divided by the number of arcseconds it slides against the stars in one hour.

On 14 November 2003 Brown's team saw a dot move 4.6 arcseconds in 3.1 hours.[^brown2004] That is 1.48 arcseconds an hour, so $R \approx 150 \div 1.48 = 101$ au, further than anything yet seen in the Solar System. For 1992 QB1, 3 arcseconds an hour gives 50 au. The true distances were 89.6 au and 40.9 au.[^horizons] The rule runs 10 to 20 per cent high, because the object also moves along its own orbit, in the same direction as the Earth, which trims its backward slide. For deciding on the first night whether to ask for more telescope time, that is plenty.

### How big?

A dot has no size, only brightness, and brightness depends on how much area catches sunlight and how much of that light the surface throws back. That fraction is the albedo, $p$: a few per cent for a surface as dark as charcoal, most of the light for fresh snow. Astronomers express brightness as an absolute magnitude, $H$, the brightness the object would have 1 au from both the Sun and the Earth. Magnitudes run backwards, so bigger numbers mean fainter, and every 5 magnitudes is a factor of 100.

$$D = \frac{1{,}329\ \text{km}}{\sqrt{p}} \times 10^{-H/5}$$

In words: the diameter equals 1,329 km divided by the square root of the albedo, multiplied by ten to the power of minus one fifth of the absolute magnitude. The square root is there because reflected light grows with area, and area grows as the diameter squared.

Take Arrokoth, the small world New Horizons flew past in 2019. Its absolute magnitude is 11.06,[^sbdb] so $10^{-11.06/5} = 10^{-2.21} = 0.00614$. If it were as dark as charcoal, $p = 0.04$ and $\sqrt{p} = 0.2$, giving $D = 6{,}645 \times 0.00614 = 41$ km. If it reflected 15 per cent, $\sqrt{0.15} = 0.387$ and $D = 21$ km. The same dot could be one size or twice that. New Horizons measured the albedo as 0.165, for which the formula gives 20 km; the real object has two lobes, 22 km and 14 km across, and a ball of the same volume would be 17 to 18 km wide.[^stern2019] Not bad for a dot. Every size worked out from brightness alone hides a guess about the albedo, and that guess is where the next part of the story went wrong.

## The tenth planet that wasn't

From November 2001 Mike Brown of Caltech, Chad Trujillo and David Rabinowitz used the 48-inch Samuel Oschin Schmidt telescope on Palomar Mountain to sweep wide areas of sky for the rare bright objects that deeper, narrower searches missed.[^brown2004] To cut the false alarms, the software ignored anything slower than 1.5 arcseconds an hour. On 21 October 2003 the telescope caught an object moving 1.42, about 106 au away by the rule of thumb, and threw it out.[^brown2005] After Sedna, the team re-ran two years of data with a separate search for slow movers, and at 11:20 am on 5 January 2005 the object appeared.[^brown2005][^brownlila] It was 97 au from the Sun, near the far end of a 560-year orbit tilted 44 degrees to the planets.[^brown2005]

Brown had not meant to announce it yet. But in late July 2005 a team in Spain announced another bright object his group had been tracking, now called Haumea, and he learned that his telescope logs had been readable on the web.[^brownlila] He announced the new one on 29 July.[^mpec2005] Its brightness meant that even if it reflected every photon that hit it, it would be as big as Pluto.[^brown2005] "We are 100 percent confident that this is the first object bigger than Pluto ever found in the outer solar system," Brown said, and NASA called it the tenth planet.[^jpl2005]

The new object had a moon, Dysnomia, which goes round every 15.774 days at 37,350 km. By Newton's rule that gives it a mass of $1.67 \times 10^{22}$ kg, 27 per cent more than Pluto.[^brownschaller2007] Size took longer. In November 2010 it passed in front of a faint star, and telescopes in Chile timed how long the star vanished: 2,326 km across, give or take 12, with an albedo of 0.96, brighter than fresh snow.[^eso1142][^sicardy2011] A snowball that shiny looks big from far away. In 2015 New Horizons measured Pluto at 2,370 km, later refined to 2,377.[^nasa2015size][^nasaplutofacts] The newcomer is heavier but slightly smaller, so it must be rockier inside.

By then the naming fight was over. On 24 August 2006, the last day of the International Astronomical Union's General Assembly in Prague, members voted that a planet orbits the Sun, is massive enough for its own gravity to pull it round, and has cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit of other bodies. Pass the first two tests but not the third, and you are a dwarf planet. The resolution making Pluto the prototype of a new class of objects beyond Neptune passed by 237 votes to 157, with 17 abstentions.[^iau0603][^iau2006res] Three weeks later the tenth planet that wasn't got its name: Eris, the Greek goddess of strife.[^brownlila]

::: myth Pluto stopped being a planet because it is too small.
The IAU definition has no size limit. Pluto passes the test of being massive enough to be round. It fails the test of having cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit: it shares its region with thousands of other Kuiper belt objects and crosses Neptune's orbit, while each of the eight planets dominates its own path round the Sun.[^iau2006res][^sbdb]
:::

## Sedna and the inner Oort cloud

The slow dot from 14 November 2003 was Sedna, and its orbit made even less sense. Everything else known beyond Neptune either came close enough to Neptune to be pushed around by it, or lived far out where passing stars do the pushing.[^brown2004] Sedna does neither. Its closest point to the Sun is 76 au, well clear of Neptune; its furthest, about 1,000 au, is too close in for the stars or the Milky Way's tides to matter much.[^jewittsedna] It takes about 12,700 years to go round and will be closest to the Sun in about 2075.[^sbdb]

Something lifted its orbit away from the planets and has since stopped. Brown's team listed the suspects: a planet not yet found, an unusually close pass by another star, or the Sun's birth in a crowded cluster of young stars. In the two they thought likeliest, Sedna belongs to an inner Oort cloud that begins much closer to the Sun than anyone had expected.[^brown2004] And a body around a thousand kilometres across could not have grown out there; it must have formed nearer the planets and been thrown out.[^jewittsedna]

It is no longer alone. 2012 VP113 never comes within 80 au of the Sun.[^trujillo2014] Leleākūhonua swings between 65 and 2,630 au, far enough out to feel the galaxy's tides,[^sheppard2019][^sbdb] and a 2022 review calls it the likeliest directly detected member of the Oort cloud.[^kaibvolk2022] In 2025 a team using Japan's Subaru telescope reported 2023 KQ14, nicknamed Ammonite, which stays beyond 66 au.[^chen2025] Some astronomers read the way these orbits line up as the fingerprint of an unseen planet; [Clockwork and chaos](#/learn/clockwork-and-chaos) covers that argument.

## Pluto up close

New Horizons left Cape Canaveral on 19 January 2006 at 58,536 km/h, the fastest launch of any spacecraft, and a pass by Jupiter in 2007 cut three years from the trip.[^nasanh] On its upper deck rode about an ounce of the ashes of Clyde Tombaugh, who had died nine years earlier.[^collectspace] At 11:49 UT on 14 July 2015 it passed 13,691 km from Pluto's centre, about 12,500 km above the surface.[^stern2015][^nasanh] Radio signals took 4 hours 25 minutes each way,[^horizons] and the team heard nothing until 00:53 UT on 15 July, when a 15-minute burst of status messages confirmed that the flyby had worked.[^nasanh]

Pluto turned out to be busy. The western half of a pale, heart-shaped region named Tombaugh Regio is a plain of frozen nitrogen about 1,000 km across, Sputnik Planitia, divided into cells tens of kilometres wide that look like slowly churning ice.[^stern2015][^nasanh] It has no craters bigger than 10 km, so it has been resurfaced within the last few hundred million years and may still be changing. Around it stand mountains of water ice 2 to 3 km high, and above it hangs a haze about 150 km deep, in an atmosphere with about a hundred-thousandth of the Earth's surface pressure.[^stern2015] In July 2026 a study of the images argued that dark streaks on the northern glacier are now and then wetted by liquid nitrogen seeping up from underneath. If so, liquid has flowed on Pluto recently.[^nasa2026liquid]

### Arrokoth

Past Pluto, New Horizons still had fuel, and the team aimed it at a small object that the Hubble Space Telescope had found for the purpose in 2014.[^stern2019] It flew by at 05:33:22 UT on 1 January 2019, 3,538 km from the object's centre, 43.3 au from the Sun and 6.6 billion km from the Earth.[^stern2019][^horizons] Arrokoth looked like a pancake stuck to a lumpy ball: lobes about 22 by 20 by 7 km and 14 by 14 by 10 km, about 36 km end to end.[^stern2019] Bodies in its part of the belt now collide at around 300 m/s, fast enough to shatter both. Arrokoth's lobes show no crushing, so they touched at a few metres per second or less.[^stern2019] The best explanation is that the pair formed together in a collapsing cloud of pebbles when the Sun was young and gently spiralled into each other.[^mckinnon2020] On its near-circular, barely tilted orbit it has probably never been heated: a building block of the planets, left over.

## Where the Sun's wind stops

The Sun blows a thin stream of charged particles in every direction. Near the plane of the planets this solar wind moves at about 400 km/s, and from the Sun's poles at up to about 800 km/s.[^swpc] As it spreads through ever-larger spheres its push weakens with the square of the distance: at 100 au it is 10,000 times feebler than at the Earth. Between the stars is a thin gas that pushes back, and somewhere the two balance. The wind moves faster than pressure waves can travel through it, so it cannot ease off gradually. It slows abruptly, from faster than sound to slower, at a termination shock,[^nasavim] rather like the fast, thin sheet of water spreading under a kitchen tap that suddenly jumps up into a thicker, slower ring. Beyond lies the heliosheath of slowed wind, and at its outer edge, where the Sun's plasma meets the galaxy's, the heliopause. Inside is the heliosphere.

The two Voyagers, launched in 1977 to visit the giant planets,[^voyfast] met the termination shock in different places: Voyager 1 in December 2004 at 94 au, Voyager 2 in August 2007 at 84 au, heading another way.[^nasavim] The bubble is not a neat sphere.

Voyager 1 reached the heliopause on 25 August 2012, 121.6 au out.[^nasavim][^horizons] Particles from inside the bubble all but vanished and cosmic rays from the galaxy jumped.[^stone2013] Yet the magnetic field had not changed direction, as many expected it would at the boundary, and the instrument built to measure the plasma directly had stopped working in 1980.[^nasavim][^stone2013] Papers in the summer of 2013 described the region as a magnetic highway, still inside the heliosphere.[^stone2013]

The Sun settled it. A coronal mass ejection, a burst of plasma thrown off in March 2012, reached Voyager 1 in April 2013 and set the surrounding gas ringing. The pitch depends on the density, and the plasma wave instrument heard gas more than 40 times denser than Voyager 2 had found in the heliosheath: interstellar plasma.[^jpl2013][^gurnett2013] "We literally jumped out of our seats when we saw these oscillations in our data," said Don Gurnett, who led the plasma wave team.[^jpl2013] Voyager 2, whose plasma instrument still worked, crossed on 5 November 2018 at 119.0 au and measured the change directly.[^nasavim][^stone2019][^horizons] [Light takes time](#/learn/light-takes-time) follows Voyager 1 to November 2026, when its signals will need a full day to reach us.

## Escape

Belonging to the Solar System is a matter of speed, not place. An object near the Sun has kinetic energy from its motion and a debt of gravitational energy from sitting in the Sun's pit. If it moves fast enough to pay the debt in full, it coasts away and never returns. If not, it is on a closed orbit, however long.

$$v_{\text{esc}} = 42.1\ \text{km/s} \times \sqrt{\frac{1\ \text{au}}{r}}$$

In words: the speed needed to escape the Sun's gravity from a distance $r$ is 42.1 km/s divided by the square root of that distance measured in au.

At the Earth's distance you need 42.1 km/s. At Jupiter, 5.2 au out, you need $42.1 \div \sqrt{5.2} = 18.5$ km/s. Voyager 1 is 171.85 au from the Sun, where the escape speed is $42.1 \div 13.1 = 3.2$ km/s, and it is doing 16.88 km/s.[^horizons] It is never coming back. At 100,000 au, out in the Oort cloud, the escape speed is only 133 m/s, slower than an airliner, which is why a passing star can nudge a comet there out of the Solar System or down towards the Sun.

Anything faster than escape speed keeps some speed even when it is very far away. Call that leftover speed $v_\infty$. Because energy is conserved, the speed at any point follows a Pythagoras-like rule.

$$v^2 = v_{\text{esc}}^2 + v_\infty^2$$

In words: the square of an object's speed at any point equals the square of the local escape speed plus the square of the speed it will have left when it is very far from the Sun.

For Voyager 1, $v_\infty = \sqrt{16.88^2 - 3.21^2} = \sqrt{284.9 - 10.3} = 16.6$ km/s, or 3.5 au a year, close to what it is doing now.[^voyfast] At that pace Proxima Centauri, the nearest star, 268,500 au away, would take about 75,000 years, if Voyager were heading that way, which it is not. [Rockets to the stars](#/learn/rockets-to-the-stars) explains why going faster is so hard.

## The cloud nobody has seen

Long-period comets are the clue to the outermost edge. They arrive from all directions, on orbits so stretched that the Sun is barely holding on. In 1932 the Estonian astronomer Ernst Öpik, then at Harvard, worked out how passing stars would tug on comets like these.[^opik1932][^mactutoropik] In January 1950 Jan Oort at Leiden took 19 comets whose orbits were known well enough to trace back to their shape before the planets disturbed them. Ten had come from more than 20,000 au away.[^oort1950] He argued that the Sun is surrounded by a cloud of about $10^{11}$ comets stretching from 50,000 to 150,000 au, weighing between a tenth and a hundredth of the Earth, and that passing stars jostle a few of them onto paths that fall towards the Sun.[^oort1950] Fred Whipple, who took up meteor science because of Öpik, always called it the Öpik-Oort cloud.[^mactutoropik]

Seventy-six years later nobody has seen it directly. A comet shines by reflected sunlight, so it fades twice over as it recedes: less sunlight reaches it, and less of the reflection reaches us. Move a comet from 40 au to 4,000 au and it becomes $100^4$, a hundred million times, fainter. Leleākūhonua is the best candidate so far for a cloud member we have actually photographed; the rest of the evidence is still the comets themselves, which suggest the cloud holds about 700 to 800 billion comet-sized bodies.[^kaibvolk2022] NASA puts its inner edge between 2,000 and 5,000 au and its outer edge somewhere between 10,000 and 100,000 au.[^nasaoort] At 100,000 au you would be more than a third of the way to Proxima Centauri.

The stars that stir the cloud are real. About 70,000 years ago Scholz's star, a pair of small, dim stars, passed within about 52,000 au of the Sun.[^mamajek2015] In roughly 1.3 million years Gliese 710 will come to about 14,000 au, with a 90 per cent chance of passing between 10,700 and 17,400 au. Of all the close passes found in the Gaia satellite's data, it will give the Oort cloud the hardest shove.[^bailerjones2018][^berski2016]

## Visitors from other stars

If passing stars can knock comets out of our cloud, other stars must be losing theirs, and some should drift through the Solar System. On 19 October 2017 Rob Weryk at the University of Hawaii picked out a fast-moving dot in images from the Pan-STARRS 1 telescope on Haleakalā.[^jpl2017] It had already passed closest to the Sun on 9 September, 0.26 au out, and come within 24 million km of the Earth on 14 October.[^jpl2017][^issi2019] Its orbit had an eccentricity of 1.20, and anything above 1 is not a closed orbit at all; it had come in at 26.4 km/s.[^issi2019] "This is the most extreme orbit I have ever seen," said Davide Farnocchia of JPL.[^jpl2017] It was named 'Oumuamua, a Hawaiian name for a messenger that arrives first, from far away.[^iau2017]

It was odd. Its brightness swung up and down as it tumbled, so it is either very long or very flat, with one dimension at least six times another. It showed no gas and no dust, yet it pulled away from the Sun slightly faster than gravity alone allows, as a comet does when jets of gas push it.[^issi2019][^micheli2018] The acceleration is still not explained. The suggestion that it was an alien light sail does not fit the way its brightness changed.[^issi2019]

::: myth The famous picture of 'Oumuamua shows what it looks like.
The long, dark-red rock that illustrated the news in 2017 is an artist's impression.[^eso1737] Every telescope that saw 'Oumuamua saw a single point of light. Its shape was worked out from how its brightness changed as it turned, and that allows either a long cigar or a flattened disc.[^issi2019]
:::

The second visitor was found by one man with a telescope he built himself. On 30 August 2019 Gennadiy Borisov spotted a comet with his 0.65 m instrument, in a patch of sky close to the Sun that the big surveys barely watched.[^jewittseligman2023] 2I/Borisov had a coma and a tail like any comet, but an orbital eccentricity of 3.36 and an incoming speed of about 32 km/s.[^guzik2020] It gave off about as much carbon monoxide as water; in most of our comets carbon monoxide runs at a few per cent of the water.[^jewittseligman2023]

The third was found on 1 July 2025 by the ATLAS survey telescope at Río Hurtado in Chile.[^seligman2025][^nasa3i] 3I/ATLAS came in from the direction of Sagittarius, towards the centre of the galaxy, with an eccentricity of 6.14 and an incoming speed of about 58 km/s.[^seligman2025][^nasa3ifaq] It passed closest to the Sun at the end of October 2025, 1.36 au out and on the far side from the Earth, and flew within 0.19 au of Mars, where the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter and the Perseverance rover photographed it.[^sbdb][^seligman2025][^nasa3i] Hubble put its icy nucleus somewhere between 440 m and 5.6 km across.[^nasa3ifaq] The escape rule checks out: at 1.36 au the escape speed is 36.2 km/s, so at its closest it was doing $\sqrt{36.2^2 + 58^2} = 68.4$ km/s, or 246,000 km/h, exactly NASA's figure.[^nasa3ifaq]

Its chemistry is the strange part. Webb found it unusually rich in carbon dioxide and, for the first time in an interstellar object, detected methane.[^belyakov2026] In September 2026 a Webb team reported about 30 times as much deuterium, heavy hydrogen, as in the Solar System's comets and very little carbon-13, which points to a very cold birthplace early in the galaxy's history. They estimate it may have formed 10 to 12 billion years ago, more than twice as long ago as the Sun.[^nasawebb2026]

Three in eight years sounds rare, but they are probably everywhere. From how hard 'Oumuamua was to find, astronomers estimate about one such object in every 10 cubic au of space. A sphere the size of Neptune's orbit holds about 113,000 cubic au, so about 10,000 of them are inside it at any moment, nearly all too small and faint to notice.[^jewittseligman2023]

If you mean the planets, the edge is Neptune, 4 light-hours out. If you mean the Sun's wind, it is the heliopause, about 17 light-hours out, and two spacecraft have already crossed it. If you mean the Sun's gravity, it is the outer Oort cloud, perhaps more than a light-year away, where the pull of the rest of the galaxy starts to win. Nobody knows exactly where that is, because nobody has seen the cloud.

## What comes next

### The Rubin Observatory's survey

**Status:** running since the end of June 2026. The Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile began its ten-year Legacy Survey of Space and Time, announced on 30 June 2026. Its 3,200-megapixel camera takes an image about every 40 seconds and will return to each part of the southern sky about 800 times.[^rubin2616] In test data it found more than 11,000 new asteroids in six weeks, about 380 of them beyond Neptune, including two whose orbits reach about 1,000 au.[^rubin2608] It is the best chance yet to find more bodies like Sedna and to settle whether a large planet hides out there; as of September 2026 none has been reported.

### Comet Interceptor

**Status:** under construction. ESA gives the launch as late 2028 or 2029, sharing a rocket with its Ariel exoplanet telescope; the date could slip. Three spacecraft, one of them built by the Japanese space agency JAXA, will wait 1.5 million km from the Earth for a comet arriving from the Oort cloud for the first time, then split up to photograph it from three sides at once.[^esaci][^esaci2][^jones2024]

### The Voyagers' last years

**Status:** both working, on dwindling power. Each spacecraft runs on a generator driven by the heat of decaying plutonium and loses about 4 watts a year.[^voylecp] Voyager 1 has two science instruments left, the magnetometer and the plasma wave instrument, after its low-energy particle detector was switched off on 17 April 2026; Voyager 2 has three.[^voywhere] In the summer of 2026 engineers turned off a group of Voyager 2's powered devices and switched on lower-power substitutes all at once, a manoeuvre they call "the Big Bang", which should keep its three instruments running for at least another year. The same swap for Voyager 1 is planned for the coming months.[^voybigbang] Voyager 1 will be one light-day from the Earth on 18 November 2026, and Voyager 2 in November 2035.[^lightday]

### New Horizons in the outer Kuiper belt

**Status:** extended mission. NASA agreed in 2023 to keep New Horizons working until it leaves the Kuiper belt, expected in 2028 or 2029, with the emphasis on the Sun's outer heliosphere.[^nhextend] It woke on 23 June 2026 from a 321-day hibernation, 9.5 billion km from the Earth, and is now 65.7 au from the Sun.[^nhwake][^horizons] Its dust counter, named after Venetia Burney, recorded more dust between 45 and 55 au than models predicted, a hint that the Kuiper belt may stretch to 80 au or beyond. NASA says the spacecraft has the power and propellant to keep going into the 2040s, past 100 au.[^nhdust]

### Mapping the bubble from home

**Status:** operating. NASA's Interstellar Mapping and Acceleration Probe, IMAP, launched on 24 September 2025 and works from a point about 1.6 million km sunward of the Earth, with ten instruments that map the edge of the heliosphere from afar.[^nasaimap] An earlier mission, IBEX, found a narrow bright ribbon across the sky in the fast neutral atoms that stream in from the boundary.[^mccomas2009][^mcnutt2021] The overall shape of the bubble is still argued over; one 2020 study suggested it is smaller and rounder than the long-tailed shape usually drawn.[^opher2020]

### Interstellar Probe

**Status:** a concept study, with no approved launch date. A 2021 study for NASA by the Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory describes a probe that would swing past Jupiter and leave the Solar System at about 7 au a year, twice Voyager 1's speed. Launched in the window it studied, 28 August to 18 September 2036, it would reach 100 au in under 15 years and pass 1,000 au after about 145.[^mcnutt2021] The survey that sets US priorities in solar and space physics, released at the end of 2024, recommended two other flagship missions, a constellation called Links and a Solar Polar Orbiter.[^decadal2025][^spacenews2024] Whether Interstellar Probe flies in the 2030s, or at all, is open.

### Open questions

Several questions have no date attached: whether a large planet lies beyond Neptune; where the Kuiper belt really ends, and whether a second belt lies further out, as New Horizons' dust counter hints; what pushed 'Oumuamua; and how many bodies the Oort cloud holds, and where its inner and outer edges are. The best hope for most of them is Rubin's growing catalogue, and whatever turns up next from another star.
