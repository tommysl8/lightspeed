On 23 October 1838 Friedrich Wilhelm Bessel, director of the observatory at Königsberg in Prussia, wrote a long letter to John Herschel in London. He wrote it in German, he explained, so that his meaning would not come out blurred. The opening was modest: "Having succeeded in obtaining a long-looked-for result", he thought Herschel might be interested, and might pass it on to other friends of astronomy if he judged it important enough.[^bessel1838]

Page after page of tables followed. They gave the position of a faint double star in the Swan, 61 Cygni, measured against two fainter stars beside it on 85 nights for one and 98 for the other, between August 1837 and October 1838. Over the year the double star had traced a tiny oval on the sky, in step with the Earth's trip round the Sun. The size of that oval gave Bessel a parallax of 0.3136 arcseconds, the angle covered by a coin 2.3 cm across seen from 15 km away. It put 61 Cygni 657,700 times further from us than the Sun. Light, he added, takes 10.3 years to make the trip.[^bessel1838]

Bessel was 54. He had tried for this number once before, around 1818, and failed.[^mactutorbessel][^fernie1975b] So had everyone since Robert Hooke in 1669, and the failure went back much further. For two thousand years the stars' refusal to shift had been one of the best arguments that the Earth does not move at all.

## A sky that would not move

Aristotle, teaching in Athens in the fourth century BCE, had a simple test for anyone who claimed the Earth was moving. A moving observer should see the stars shift. In *On the Heavens* he put it bluntly: if the Earth moved, "there would have to be passings and turnings of the fixed stars. Yet no such thing is observed."[^aristotle] He was right about the observation, and would stay right for two thousand years.

The one Greek astronomer who put the Sun in the middle had an answer ready. Aristarchus of Samos, in the third century BCE, set the Earth on a circle round the Sun and, according to Archimedes, made the sphere of the stars so vast that the Earth's whole orbit was, by comparison, a point.[^heath1897] The shift was there; the stars were simply too far away for anyone to see it. Nicolaus Copernicus gave the same answer in 1543: the absence of any such effect among the fixed stars proved how enormously high they were.[^graney2011]

Tycho Brahe, the finest observer of the sixteenth century, put that answer to the test. At his observatory on the island of Hven he could pin a star's position to better than an arcminute, a sixtieth of a degree, and he saw no shift. To hide one, Copernican stars had to be nearly 7,000 times as far away as the Sun, or further.[^graney2011] Tycho had also measured the stars' sizes, or thought he had: a first-magnitude star looked about 2 arcminutes across.[^fernie1975a] Anything that looks 2 arcminutes wide from that distance must be hundreds of times the width of the Sun. With the Earth standing still, the same stars could sit just beyond Saturn and be about as big as the Sun.[^graney2011] Tycho judged a universe of monstrous stars absurd and built his own system, in which the planets circle the Sun and the Sun circles a motionless Earth.

His flaw was invisible to him. Stars have no size the eye can measure; the little discs we seem to see are made by the eye and the air. Jeremiah Horrocks saw the hint on 19 March 1637, when the Moon passed in front of the Pleiades and the stars blinked out at its edge instantly, instead of fading as real discs would.[^fernie1975a] Stars are points. Once that sank in, the size argument collapsed, and the missing parallax stopped being an objection to a moving Earth and became a measurement nobody could make.

::: myth People rejected a moving Earth to protect religion, not because of evidence.
Tycho's case was built on measurement: no parallax down to the limit of his instruments, and star sizes that would make Copernican stars hundreds of times wider than the Sun. It was some Copernicans of his day who reached for theology, arguing that God could make stars as big as He liked, and Tycho rejected that as no answer at all.[^graney2011]
:::

## A very thin triangle

Hold a finger up at arm's length and look at it with one eye, then the other. It jumps against the background. Move the finger further away and the jump shrinks. Stellar parallax is the same trick with the Earth's orbit as the pair of eyes. In January and July the Earth is on opposite sides of the Sun, two astronomical units apart. One au is 149,597,870.7 km, close to the Earth's average distance from the Sun; [How big is the Solar System?](#/learn/how-big-is-the-solar-system) tells how it was measured.[^iauunits] A nearby star seen from those two places shifts against much more distant stars behind it, and over a year it traces a small ellipse. Astronomers define the parallax $p$ as half of the full shift: the angle that one au makes when seen from the star.

Angles this small make trigonometry easy. Measure them in radians: wrap the radius of a circle around its edge and the angle it covers is one radian, 57.3 degrees, or 206,265 arcseconds. For a long thin triangle, the short side divided by the long side is the angle in radians. Astronomers built a unit out of this. A parsec (a *par*allax of one arc*sec*ond) is the distance at which one au spans exactly one arcsecond. Since one arcsecond is 1/206,265 of a radian, one parsec is 206,265 au; the International Astronomical Union now fixes it at exactly $648{,}000/\pi$ au.[^iau2015] That comes to 30.86 million million km, or 3.26 light-years.

$$d = \frac{1}{p}$$

In words: a star's distance in parsecs is one divided by its parallax in arcseconds.

Bessel's 0.3136 arcseconds gives $d = 1 \div 0.3136 = 3.19$ pc. Multiply by 206,265 and you get his 657,700 au; multiply by 3.26 and you get 10.4 light-years. Gaia's modern parallax for 61 Cygni, 0.2860 arcseconds, puts it at 3.50 pc or 11.40 light-years.[^simbad] Bessel's first-ever measurement was 10% too big.

Because $d$ and $p$ are inverses, every step outwards makes the job harder. Proxima Centauri, the nearest star, has the biggest parallax of any star, 0.768 arcseconds: a 2.3 cm coin seen from 6 km. A star 100 parsecs away has a parallax of 0.01 arcseconds, and one at 1,000 parsecs, 0.001.

The other unit you will meet is the light-year: the distance light travels in a year of 365.25 days, which the IAU gives as 9,460,730,472,580.8 km, or 63,241 au.[^iauunits] [Light takes time](#/learn/light-takes-time) tells how we learned the speed that goes into it.

## Two centuries of near misses

Robert Hooke was probably the first to try with a telescope. In July, August and October 1669, at Gresham College in London, he aimed a 36-foot telescope straight up at Gamma Draconis, a star that passes almost overhead there, where the air bends starlight least. In 1674 he announced a parallax of 27 to 30 arcseconds.[^fernie1975b][^perryman2012] The true value is 0.021 arcseconds, more than a thousand times smaller.[^hip2007] John Flamsteed, the first Astronomer Royal, claimed 40 arcseconds for Polaris around 1698.[^fernie1975b] Both men had seen real movements and misread them.

James Bradley found out what those movements were. In December 1725, watching Gamma Draconis from a friend's house at Kew, he saw it drift the wrong way for parallax. The cause, he announced in 1729, was aberration: because the Earth moves at 30 km/s, starlight arrives at a slight slant, as rain seems to slant when you run through it. [Light takes time](#/learn/light-takes-time) tells that story. He had found the first direct proof that the Earth moves while looking for a different one. He also concluded that the parallax of Gamma Draconis had to be under about one arcsecond, or he would have seen it.[^herschel1842][^fernie1975b]

William Herschel, who found Uranus in 1781, brought a better idea. Measuring a star's absolute position means fighting the air, the sag of the instrument and aberration all at once. Measuring one star against another close beside it cancels most of that. Galileo had suggested it in 1632: find a bright star with a faint one near it, assume the faint one is far behind, and watch the gap change through the year.[^fernie1975b] In a paper read to the Royal Society on 6 December 1781 Herschel admitted that on the distances of the stars "we remain in a great measure still in the dark", and reckoned that the nearest star could be no closer than 40,000 diameters of the Earth's orbit.[^herschel1782] That is 80,000 au. The truth for the nearest star is about 270,000 au.

He published two catalogues of close pairs for the purpose. When he came back to them twenty years later, many had moved, but not with the yearly back-and-forth of parallax. The two stars of Castor had turned around each other. In 1803 Herschel told the Royal Society that many double stars were real pairs bound by their mutual gravity, the first evidence that Newton's gravity works among the stars.[^herschel1803][^perryman2012] The English astronomer John Michell had argued from probability in 1767 that close pairs were far too common to be chance line-ups; Herschel was pointed to the paper and pressed on anyway.[^fernie1975b] A real pair shows no relative parallax, because both stars are at the same distance. The method had been hunting in exactly the wrong place.

The early nineteenth century brought a run of claims: 4 arcseconds for Sirius from Giuseppe Piazzi in Palermo, about 1 for Vega from John Brinkley in Dublin. John Pond, the Astronomer Royal, found nothing at Greenwich and said so, paper after paper, in a dispute with Brinkley that ran for more than a decade.[^fernie1975b] Nobody knew how small a shift to look for.

## The race of 1838

What finally worked was better glass and a better choice of star. Almost everyone had chosen bright stars, assuming bright meant near. Wilhelm Struve, at the observatory in Dorpat (now Tartu, in Estonia), argued in 1837 that a star which drifts quickly across the sky was a better bet. Every star has some motion of its own through space, and the closer it is, the faster that motion carries it across our view.[^perryman2012][^fernie1975b] Edmond Halley had discovered this drift, called proper motion, in 1718, when he found that Sirius, Arcturus and Aldebaran had moved since the Greeks measured them.[^halley1718]

Struve also had a superb telescope, a 24 cm refractor by Joseph Fraunhofer, the world's largest when Dorpat got it in 1824.[^fernie1975b][^perryman2012] He chose Vega, which is bright, moves fast and has a faint star close by, and measured the gap between the two seventeen times from 1835 on. His answer, 0.125 arcseconds, appeared in 1837 in the last chapter of a huge catalogue of double stars.[^fernie1975b][^perryman2012] A copy of the book was presented to the Royal Astronomical Society in London on 10 November 1837. At the same meeting the Astronomer Royal, George Airy, reported 184 observations of Vega made at Greenwich during 1836, and concluded that its parallax was too small for the best instruments to detect.[^mnras1837][^fernie1975b]

Struve was right, and Airy wrong: the modern parallax of Vega is 0.130 arcseconds.[^hip2007] But Struve had offered the number cautiously, and a couple of years later, with nearly a hundred observations, he revised it to 0.2619 arcseconds, twice the true value.[^fernie1975b][^perryman2012] He had had it, and let it go.

Bessel said himself that Struve's Vega result gave him the push to try again.[^bessel1838][^fernie1975b] He chose 61 Cygni because it had the largest proper motion he knew of, 5.123 arcseconds a year, and because its two stars travelled together and so had to be at the same distance.[^bessel1838][^fernie1975b] His instrument was a heliometer made by Fraunhofer: a telescope whose lens had been sawn in half, so that sliding one half past the other made two overlapping images of the sky. Slide until one star sits on top of another, read the screw, and you have the angle between them, far more precisely than the spider-thread micrometers most others used.[^perryman2012][^fernie1975b]

He had begun in September 1834 and been interrupted three times: three months measuring the swing of a pendulum in Berlin, the return of Halley's Comet in 1835, which, he wrote, took every clear night, and a year of calculations for a survey of the shape of the Earth.[^bessel1838] From August 1837 he measured the double star against two faint neighbours, one roughly at right angles to the line of the pair and one nearly along it, usually repeating each measurement sixteen times a night. His result came with an uncertainty of 0.0202 arcseconds, less than a fifteenth of the parallax, and the shifts followed the Earth round its orbit as closely as anyone could expect for an effect so small.[^bessel1838] That pattern, more than the number, is what convinced people.[^perryman2012]

Thomas Henderson had the data first and sat on it. A Scottish lawyer who had made himself into an astronomer, he took charge of the observatory at the Cape of Good Hope in 1832, loathed it, called it the Dismal Swamp, and left after about thirteen months.[^fernie1975b][^lindahall] While he was there he measured Alpha Centauri, a bright double star that can only be seen from the south, again and again, with an instrument that turned out to be faulty.[^glass2007] In 1833 Lieutenant Manuel Johnson, observing on St Helena, told him that Alpha Centauri had a large proper motion.[^glass2007] He went home to become the first Astronomer Royal for Scotland. His measurements hinted at a parallax, but he held back until a second set could be checked, and only after Struve's and Bessel's announcements did he finish the work: 1.16 arcseconds. His paper was read in London in January 1839. He had not even been looking for parallax.[^henderson1839][^glass2007]

The Royal Astronomical Society gave Bessel its gold medal. Presenting it on 12 February 1841, John Herschel said the old barrier had been "almost simultaneously overleaped at three different points", by Bessel, Henderson and Struve, and called it "the greatest and most glorious triumph which practical astronomy has ever witnessed".[^herschel1842] In the same speech he said that if Henderson's successor at the Cape, Thomas Maclear, confirmed the Alpha Centauri result with better instruments, the credit for the first parallax would be Henderson's.[^herschel1842] Maclear did confirm it, in 1842, but the credit stayed with Bessel, whose observations had been designed for the job.[^fernie1975b][^glass2007] Henderson's number was also further off: the modern parallax of Alpha Centauri is 0.751 arcseconds, so his was 55% too big, against Bessel's 10%.[^akeson2021] Henderson died in 1844, aged 45.[^lindahall] Bessel died two years later.[^mactutorbessel]

## Stars as suns

Long before anyone measured a parallax there was a shortcut. Assume a star is a copy of the Sun, compare how bright it looks, and work out how far away a Sun would have to be to look that faint. The Scottish mathematician James Gregory tried it on Sirius in 1668 and got 83,190 times the Sun's distance. In a paper published in 1829 William Wollaston compared Sirius with sunlight glinting off a glass bead a tenth of an inch across, seen from 210 feet away, and got 141,421 times.[^fernie1975a]

The physics behind the shortcut is the inverse-square law. Light leaving a star spreads over the surface of an ever-growing sphere, and a sphere's area grows with the square of its radius. Go twice as far and the same light is spread over four times the area.

$$b = \frac{L}{4\pi d^2}$$

In words: the brightness you receive, as power per square metre, is the star's total power output divided by the area of a sphere whose radius is your distance from the star.

The Sun puts out $3.828 \times 10^{26}$ watts.[^nssdcsun] At one au, $1.496 \times 10^{11}$ m, the sphere has an area of $4\pi \times (1.496 \times 10^{11})^2 = 2.81 \times 10^{23}$ square metres, so $b = 3.828 \times 10^{26} \div 2.81 \times 10^{23} = 1{,}361$ watts per square metre, the figure NASA gives for sunlight arriving at the Earth.[^nssdcearth] From Alpha Centauri, 274,700 au away, the same sunlight is $274{,}700^2 = 75$ billion times weaker.

Now run Gregory's argument with modern numbers. Sirius looks 13 billion times fainter than the Sun (the next section shows where that comes from). A twin of the Sun would have to be $\sqrt{1.3 \times 10^{10}} = 114{,}000$ au away to look that faint. Parallax puts Sirius at 544,000 au.[^hip2007] It is 4.8 times further than a Sun-twin would be, so it must give out $4.8^2 = 23$ times as much visible light as the Sun. Gregory and Wollaston had the physics right and the assumption wrong. Wollaston's number already carried the clue: it implied a parallax of about 1.5 arcseconds ($206{,}265 \div 141{,}421$) for a Sun-like Sirius, and by then observers knew the real one was under half an arcsecond, so Sirius had to outshine the Sun.[^fernie1975a][^wollaston1829]

Turned around, the law becomes a tool: measure a star's distance by parallax and its brightness with a light meter, and out comes its true power output. Stars of known power then become yardsticks far beyond the reach of parallax; see [What stars are made of](#/learn/what-stars-are-made-of) and [Island universes](#/learn/island-universes).

## Magnitudes, and a short guide to logarithms

Astronomers still measure brightness on a scale older than the telescope. Stars were sorted into six magnitudes, the first for the brightest and the sixth for the faintest the eye can see. The word meant size, because bright stars seemed bigger, and Tycho even gave each class a diameter.[^graney2011][^fernie1975a] In 1856 Norman Pogson at Oxford pinned the scale down: each magnitude is a factor of about 2.512 in brightness, chosen so that five magnitudes make a factor of 100.[^pogson1856] A smaller number means brighter, and the brightest objects go negative. Sirius is magnitude −1.46; the Sun, −26.74.[^simbad][^nssdcsun]

To use the scale you need logarithms, and one paragraph is enough. The logarithm of a number answers the question "ten to what power gives this?" So $\log_{10} 100 = 2$, because $10^2 = 100$; $\log_{10} 1{,}000{,}000 = 6$; and $\log_{10} 0.01 = -2$. Numbers that are not neat powers of ten have logarithms in between: $\log_{10} 2 = 0.301$, because $10^{0.301} = 2$. Multiplying two numbers adds their logarithms, so $\log_{10} 200 = 2 + 0.301 = 2.301$. Logarithms turn enormous ratios into small numbers, which is why astronomy is full of them.

$$m_1 - m_2 = -2.5\,\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{b_1}{b_2}\right)$$

In words: the difference between two stars' magnitudes is minus two and a half times the logarithm of the ratio of their brightnesses.

Check it against Pogson's rule: a star 100 times brighter than another has $\log_{10} 100 = 2$, and $-2.5 \times 2 = -5$, five magnitudes brighter. Now the Sun and Sirius. Their magnitudes differ by $-1.46 - (-26.74) = 25.28$, so the Sun is brighter by a factor of $10^{25.28 \div 2.5} = 10^{10.11} = 1.3 \times 10^{10}$. That is the 13 billion used for Gregory's argument.

A star's absolute magnitude, $M$, is the magnitude it would have seen from exactly 10 parsecs. Put the inverse-square law into the magnitude formula and out comes the distance modulus:

$$m - M = 5\,\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{d}{10\ \text{pc}}\right)$$

In words: how much fainter a star looks than it would from 10 parsecs is five times the logarithm of its distance measured in tens of parsecs. The five is 2.5 doubled, because brightness falls with the square of distance.

Sirius is 2.637 pc away, so $d/10\ \text{pc} = 0.2637$, whose logarithm is $-0.579$. Five times that is $-2.89$, so $M = -1.46 + 2.89 = +1.43$. The Sun's absolute magnitude is +4.83.[^nssdcsun] The gap of 3.40 magnitudes is a factor of $10^{3.40 \div 2.5} = 23$: the same answer the inverse-square law gave, as it must be.

Now look back at the Sun. From Alpha Centauri, 1.332 pc away, the Sun would be at magnitude $4.83 + 5 \log_{10} 0.1332 = 4.83 - 4.38 = 0.45$, a little fainter than Procyon. It would sit in the constellation Cassiopeia, in the direction exactly opposite to where Alpha Centauri sits in our sky.

## Glass plates and runaway stars

Photography turned parallax from a lifetime's work into a production line. Frank Schlesinger began taking parallax plates with the 40-inch refractor at Yerkes Observatory in Wisconsin in 1903, and by 1924 his General Catalogue of Stellar Parallaxes listed nearly 2,000 stars.[^perryman2012] The last great ground-based edition, from Yale in 1995, held 15,994 parallaxes for 8,112 stars, the newer ones typically good to 0.004 arcseconds.[^vanaltena1995]

Plates had a second use. Compare two taken years apart and anything that has moved stands out. In 1915 Robert Innes, director of the Union Observatory in Johannesburg, spent about 40 hours comparing a plate from 1910 with one from 1915, covering 60 square degrees around Alpha Centauri. He found a faint star 2 degrees from it, drifting across the sky at 4.87 arcseconds a year, much like Alpha Centauri.[^glass2007] In 1917 J. G. E. G. Voûte, a Dutch volunteer at the Cape observatory, measured its parallax at 0.755 arcseconds, the same as Alpha Centauri's.[^voute1917] Innes, with a rougher value of 0.759, declared it the nearest star of all and suggested it be called Proxima Centaurus. His evidence did not really support the claim, but measurements from Johannesburg in 1928 showed he had guessed right.[^glass2007] Proxima's parallax is now known to be 0.7681 arcseconds: 4.25 light-years.[^simbad] It is a red dwarf of magnitude 11, about a hundred times fainter than the faintest star you can see unaided, and it orbits Alpha Centauri A and B once every 550,000 years or so.[^kervella2017]

In 1916 Edward Emerson Barnard, at Yerkes, reported "a small star of the 11th (photographic) magnitude which has a large proper-motion of about 10″.3 a year", heading almost due north.[^barnard1916] He had found it on his own recent photographs and then spotted it on a plate he had taken at Lick Observatory on 24 August 1894. Barnard's Star still has the largest proper motion of any known star, 10.39 arcseconds a year, enough to cross the width of the full Moon in about 180 years.[^simbad][^luhman2014]

Proper motion is an angle per year. To turn it into a speed you need the distance, and parallax supplies it.

$$v_t = 4.74\,\mu\,d$$

In words: a star's speed across our line of sight, in kilometres per second, is 4.74 times its proper motion in arcseconds per year times its distance in parsecs.

A star one parsec away that drifts one arcsecond a year is moving one au a year across our line of sight, by the definition of the parsec, and one au per year is $149{,}597{,}870.7\ \text{km} \div 31{,}557{,}600\ \text{s} = 4.74$ km/s. For Barnard's Star, $\mu = 10.39$ and $d = 1.828$ pc, so $v_t = 4.74 \times 10.39 \times 1.828 = 90$ km/s. The Doppler shift of its light adds a speed straight towards us of 110 km/s.[^simbad] Pythagoras combines the two: $\sqrt{90^2 + 110^2} = 142$ km/s. Barnard's Star is heading our way at a slant. If it keeps to a straight line, it will pass closest in about 9,700 years, at 3.8 light-years instead of today's 5.96.

## Hipparcos: measuring from above the air

On the ground, the shimmering air set the limit, and published parallaxes were often worse than they claimed to be.[^perryman2012] The French astronomer Pierre Lacroute proposed doing the work from space, with a telescope looking in two widely separated directions at once so that it could stitch together large angles right across the sky. The European Space Agency adopted the idea in 1980 as Hipparcos, a squeezed-down version of "high precision parallax collecting satellite" that also honours the Greek astronomer Hipparchus.[^perryman2012]

Hipparcos went up on an Ariane rocket in August 1989, and then its apogee boost motor failed to fire. Instead of a circular geostationary orbit it was stranded in a long ellipse that dragged it through the Van Allen radiation belts. The team rebuilt the mission around the orbit it had, and the satellite collected data from November 1989 to March 1993.[^esahip] The catalogue, published in June 1997, gave parallaxes for 118,218 stars good to about a thousandth of an arcsecond, fifty times better than anything before.[^esahip][^perryman2012] A second catalogue, named Tycho, added rougher positions for 1,058,332 stars, later extended to 2.5 million.[^esahip]

It also produced a famous wrong answer. Hipparcos put the Pleiades 120 parsecs away; other methods said about 133.[^melis2014] Either the models of young stars were wrong or the satellite was. In 2014 a team led by Carl Melis settled it with radio telescopes linked together as one instrument, measuring the parallaxes of individual Pleiades stars directly: 136.2 parsecs.[^melis2014] Gaia later found 135.8.[^babusiaux2018] The models were fine. For that patch of sky, Hipparcos had come up about 12% short.

## Gaia: two billion stars

Gaia left Kourou in French Guiana on a Soyuz-Fregat rocket on 19 December 2013 and settled into an orbit around L2, a point 1.5 million km beyond the Earth on the side away from the Sun. It spun slowly, sweeping two telescopes across the whole sky and recording every star down to magnitude 20 on a camera of nearly a billion pixels. Between 27 July 2014 and 15 January 2025 it made more than three trillion observations of about two billion objects, around 1% of the stars in the Milky Way.[^esagaiafacts] For stars brighter than magnitude 15 it measured positions to 24 microarcseconds; ESA compares that to seeing the width of a human hair from 1,000 km. Hipparcos had managed the hair from 20 km.[^esagaiafacts]

The third data release, on 13 June 2022, covered about 1.8 billion sources, with parallaxes and proper motions for 1.47 billion of them.[^esagaiadr3] For stars brighter than magnitude 15 the parallax uncertainty is about 0.02 to 0.03 thousandths of an arcsecond, rising to 0.5 at magnitude 20.[^edr3] At 0.02, a star can have its distance measured to 10% as far as 5,000 parsecs, about 16,000 light-years.

Gaia data gave a clean catalogue of 331,312 objects within 100 parsecs of the Sun.[^gcns2021] A 2021 census within 10 parsecs counted 540 stars, brown dwarfs and planets in 339 systems, and about 61% of the stars and brown dwarfs are small red dwarfs of type M.[^reyle2021] Precise positions also reveal things that give no light. In 2022 Kareem El-Badry and colleagues found a Sun-like star 480 parsecs away swinging round an invisible companion of 9.6 solar masses every 186 days: a black hole, Gaia BH1.[^elbadry2023] Gaia BH3, with 33 solar masses, turned up in 2024 while the team was checking data for the next release.[^panuzzo2024] And the motions of halo stars showed that the Milky Way swallowed a sizeable dwarf galaxy about ten billion years ago; [Our galaxy](#/learn/our-galaxy) tells that story.[^helmi2018]

On 27 March 2025 the control team at ESA's operations centre fired Gaia's thrusters for the last time, pushing it off L2 into a retirement orbit around the Sun chosen to make it unlikely to come within 10 million km of the Earth for at least a century. They switched its instruments off one by one and deliberately corrupted its software so it could never wake up and start transmitting. The names of about 1,500 people who had worked on the mission were written over part of its backup software. "We will never forget Gaia, and Gaia will never forget us," said the mission manager, Uwe Lammers.[^esafarewell]

Distances from Gaia parallaxes, except Alpha Centauri (the ALMA radio telescope), Luhman 16 and WISE 0855 (other telescopes) and Sirius (Hipparcos). The next system out, Gliese 65, is 8.7 light-years away.[^simbad][^akeson2021][^hip2007]

Two of those eight were unknown before 2013, and Kevin Luhman found both the way Innes and Barnard found theirs: by motion, in repeated images from NASA's WISE infrared satellite. Luhman 16 is a pair of brown dwarfs.[^luhman2013] WISE 0855−0714, found in 2014, is the coldest brown dwarf known; spectra from the James Webb Space Telescope put it at roughly 265 to 285 K, about −8 °C to +12 °C.[^luhman2014][^luhman2024] The census of the neighbourhood is still not finished.[^reyle2021]

Nor is it fixed. Five of the seven bright stars of the Big Dipper (Merak, Phecda, Megrez, Alioth and Mizar) belong to one family, the Ursa Major group, moving together; Dubhe and Alkaid are going their own way.[^soderblom1993] Taking the stars' measured motions forward 100,000 years, each drifts between 2.5 and 4 degrees, five to eight widths of the full Moon, and the gap from Dubhe to Megrez widens from 10 degrees to 16. The bowl pulls apart.

The nearest stars change places too. Following straight lines from their measured positions and velocities, Alpha Centauri will come closest in about 28,000 years, at 3.1 light-years, and a red dwarf called Ross 248, now 10.3 light-years away, will pass within about 3 light-years some 36,000 years from now and briefly be our nearest neighbour.[^akeson2021][^simbad] Closer calls happen. About 70,000 years ago Scholz's star, a small pair, passed 52,000 au from the Sun, probably through the outer Oort Cloud of comets.[^mamajek2015] In about 1.3 million years Gliese 710 will come within roughly 13,000 au, about a fifth of a light-year.[^bj2022] Its absolute magnitude is 8.25, so the distance modulus says that at 0.064 pc it will shine at $8.25 + 5 \log_{10} 0.0064 = -2.7$, brighter than Sirius is today. [The edges of the Solar System](#/learn/edges-of-the-solar-system) looks at what such visitors do to the comets.

## What comes next

### Gaia Data Release 4

**Status:** scheduled for 2 December 2026, as of September 2026.[^esadr4overview] It will be built from 66 months of observations and will cover about 2.8 billion sources, with a main, high-quality table of about 2 billion.[^esarelease][^esadr4] For the first time it will include the individual measurements behind each star, some 130 billion of them, along with image data, for a total of about 400 terabytes, forty times the size of the last release.[^esadr4] The individual measurements matter because a star tugged back and forth by an unseen planet or black hole betrays itself in them. An exoplanet list is among the extra data planned for the release.[^esadr4overview]

### Gaia Data Release 5

**Status:** planned for no earlier than the end of 2030.[^esarelease] It will use all ten and a half years of data, nearly twice the span of DR4. A longer baseline helps most with proper motions.

### The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope

**Status:** launched on 30 August 2026 on a Falcon Heavy and now being commissioned. NASA reported its 300-megapixel infrared camera switched on in mid-September, and science is due to begin by early 2027.[^romanblog] Infrared light passes through the dust that blinds Gaia towards the centre of the Galaxy. Before launch, astronomers estimated that Roman's repeated survey of the Galactic bulge would give precise parallaxes and proper motions for more than 50 million stars, aiming for 10 microarcseconds or better, enough to measure distances to individual bulge stars to about 9%.[^sanderson2019]

### JASMINE

**Status:** a Japanese mission concept, being studied by about 60 researchers, to measure star positions in the infrared around the Galactic centre. JAXA's Institute of Space and Astronautical Science says it aims to launch in 2032; like any launch date six years out, that could slip.[^jasmine]

### A Gaia for the infrared

**Status:** a candidate, not a mission. In May 2021 ESA's Voyage 2050 plan named the "Galactic Ecosystem with Astrometry in the Near-infrared" as one of two candidate themes for one of its three future large missions, ranked behind studying the atmospheres of temperate exoplanets. ESA was asked to study the exoplanet mission first and to turn to astrometry only if that proved unfeasible.[^voyage2050] A concept often called GaiaNIR could measure up to 8 billion stars that dust hides from Gaia.[^hobbs2021] As of September 2026 no such mission has been selected and there is no launch date.

### An open question: who else is passing by?

Gaia's second release could identify only about 15% of the stars that will pass within 5 parsecs of the Sun within 5 million years either side of today, mostly because faint stars lacked measurements of their speed towards or away from us.[^bj2018] Gaia's spectrometer measures those speeds: DR3 gave them for 33 million stars, and DR4's spectrometer table has about 312 million entries.[^esagaiadr3][^esadr4] The list of brown dwarfs and planets within 10 parsecs is also expected to grow.[^reyle2021]
