In January 1995 Didier Queloz, a PhD student at the University of Geneva, was running a planet search more or less on his own. His supervisor, Michel Mayor, had handed him the keys the previous autumn and left for a six-month sabbatical in Hawaii.[^queloz2019] The search used ELODIE, a new spectrograph (an instrument that splits starlight into its colours) on the 1.93-metre telescope of the Observatoire de Haute-Provence in southern France, and one of its 142 target stars, HD 217014 in Pegasus, better known as 51 Pegasi, was misbehaving: the lines in its spectrum were sliding back and forth by far more than the instrument's errors allowed.[^mayor2019]

His first reaction, Queloz said later, was panic. He assumed a fault in the spectrograph or in the software he had written for it, and spent days and nights checking. There was none. The star was moving towards us and away again every 4.2 days, and the only explanation he could find was a planet of roughly Jupiter's mass, so close to its star that its year was shorter than a working week.[^queloz2019][^mayor2019]

He had every reason to distrust it. The hunt for planets around other stars had already produced a string of discoveries that evaporated when someone else looked. Thirty-one years later the count stands at 6,372,[^neahome] and nearly all of them are known the way 51 Pegasi b was: from what they do to their stars.

## The planets that were not there

A planet does not simply orbit its star; both orbit their shared centre of mass, the balance point between them. The star's orbit is tiny, but in principle you can watch a nearby star trace a small loop against the more distant stars behind it. That was Peter van de Kamp's method. At Swarthmore College's Sproul Observatory in Pennsylvania he photographed nearby stars with a 24-inch (61 cm) refractor, a telescope that uses a lens rather than a mirror, year after year, and had their positions on the glass plates measured by hand.

His favourite target was Barnard's Star, which crosses the sky faster than any other star, more than 10 arcseconds a year (an arcsecond is 1/3,600 of a degree), and is the nearest star after the Alpha Centauri system.[^gatewood1973][^ribas2018] By 1962 fifty observers had taken 2,413 plates of it on 609 nights.[^vandekamp1963] In 1963 van de Kamp announced that once its steady drift and the yearly shuffle caused by Earth's own orbit (its parallax) were taken out, a slow wobble remained, with a period of 24 years. He put it down to an unseen companion of 1.6 Jupiter masses: a planet.[^vandekamp1963] In 1969 he refitted the data as two planets of about Jupiter's mass on 26- and 12-year orbits, a pair he compared with Saturn and Jupiter.[^vandekamp1969]

The wobble was 0.0245 arcseconds. On his plates one millimetre covered about 19 arcseconds of sky,[^vandekamp1963] so the loop was 1.3 micrometres in radius on the glass, about a fiftieth of the width of a human hair, pieced together over a quarter of a century with changing plates, changing observers and one ageing telescope.

In 1973 two papers took it apart. George Gatewood and Heinrich Eichhorn measured 241 plates of Barnard's Star from two other observatories on an automatic measuring machine and found no wobble. Their paper was titled "An unsuccessful search for a planetary companion of Barnard's star".[^gatewood1973] The same year John Hershey, at Sproul itself, measured 423 plates of a different star taken with the same telescope. Its position jumped by about 3.5 micrometres in 1949, exactly when the lens was given a new cell and the observers switched to a new kind of plate, and jumped again when the lens was adjusted in 1957.[^hershey1973] Hershey did not analyse Barnard's Star, but the implication was hard to miss: the telescope itself had shifted by more than twice the size of the wobble van de Kamp was chasing.

Van de Kamp was still writing about "the planetary system of Barnard's star" in 1982.[^vandekamp1982] The verdict that stuck, as the Canadian astronomer Gordon Walker later summed it up, is that a planet's pull on its star is too small to catch on photographic plates at all.[^walker2008]

## A planet around a dead star

The first planets that held up were found around the last kind of star anyone would have picked. A pulsar is the collapsed core of a star that exploded: a neutron star the size of a city, spinning fast and sweeping a radio beam past Earth once a turn. PSR B1257+12 turns 161 times a second, as regular as a very good clock.[^konacki2003] If something tugs it back and forth, its pulses arrive a little early when it is nearer to us and a little late when it is farther away.

Aleksander Wolszczan had been timing it with the 305-metre Arecibo radio telescope since 1990, and the ticks were not regular.[^konacki2003][^wolszczan1994] With Dale Frail he showed that the pattern fitted two bodies a few times Earth's mass on orbits of 66.5 and 98.2 days.[^wolszczan1992][^nea2026] The heavier one swings the pulsar round a circle about 500 km in radius, shifting its pulses by a little over a millisecond, and pulsars can be timed to millionths of a second.[^konacki2003]

Their paper came out on 9 January 1992, into a field about to be burned again. Six months earlier Matthew Bailes, Andrew Lyne and Setnam Shemar had announced a planet around another pulsar.[^bailes1991] A week after Wolszczan and Frail's paper, Lyne and Bailes withdrew theirs in a note titled "No planet orbiting PSR1829-10": the planet had come from a flaw in their own analysis.[^lyne1992] Wolszczan's planets could be tested in a way theirs could not. Their periods are close to a ratio of 3 to 2, and planets like that tug on each other in a way that can be calculated in advance. In 1994, after three more years of timing, he saw exactly the predicted effect, plus a third, much smaller body on a 25-day orbit.[^wolszczan1994] The nearly flat, nearly circular orbits suggest the planets formed from a disc of debris left around the pulsar.[^konacki2003]

## A factor of a thousand out

The other way to catch a wobbling star is to measure its speed rather than its position. Otto Struve proposed this in a short note in 1952. He saw no reason why giant planets should not orbit much closer to their stars than Jupiter does, where their pull would be far easier to detect, and suggested they might also be caught eclipsing their stars.[^struve1952][^nobel2019sci][^winn2010]

The first serious attempt came from Gordon Walker and Bruce Campbell at the University of British Columbia, who passed starlight through a cell of hydrogen fluoride gas so that the gas's sharp lines printed a ruler on top of the star's. By Walker's own account the first test, on 27 December 1978, was unsafe: the gas is toxic and attacks glass, and the heated cell's plastic windows warped.[^walker2008] From 1980 they measured the speeds of some two dozen Sun-like stars to about 13 m/s.[^campbell1988] Everyone expected planets like Jupiter, with orbits of a decade or more, so they were given three or four two-night observing runs a year. Campbell eventually left, unable to find a permanent job in astronomy, and the final analysis, in August 1995, found no strong evidence for Jupiter-mass planets around any of their stars. It went to the journal Icarus because Icarus had no page charges and the team had used up its publication funds.[^walker2008][^walker1995]

In Geneva, Michel Mayor's spectrograph ELODIE, designed with the optician André Baranne and fed by optical fibre, compared each spectrum with a mask of thousands of lines and reached 10 to 15 m/s without needing a gas cell, so it could work on fainter stars.[^mayor2019][^nobel2019sci] In spring 1994 Mayor, Queloz and Antoine Duquennoy began watching 142 stars, with seven nights on the telescope every second month. Duquennoy was killed in a car accident that June.[^mayor2019]

By the end of that first season 51 Pegasi was showing its 4.2-day wobble. Theory said a giant planet should take ten years or more to go round its star; Mayor later wrote that they were "a factor of 1,000 out!"[^mayor2019] They waited another season, knowing how many planet claims had collapsed and that dark spots on a rotating star can fake a wobble.[^mayor2019] New measurements in July 1995 landed where Queloz's orbit said they would.[^queloz2019] The paper went to Nature on 29 August, the same month Walker's negative result appeared, and the planet was announced at a conference in Florence on 6 October 1995.[^nobel2019sci]

Within weeks Geoffrey Marcy and Paul Butler, running their own search at Lick Observatory in California, had confirmed it, and so had a second American team.[^nobel2019sci][^nobelpop] In 2019 Mayor and Queloz shared half of the Nobel Prize in Physics for finding a planet around a Sun-like star.[^nobel2019]

## Weighing a planet by its wobble

Every element in a star's atmosphere absorbs light at its own wavelengths, so a star's spectrum is crossed by thousands of dark lines, a barcode of its chemistry (the subject of [What stars are made of](#/learn/what-stars-are-made-of)). When the star moves towards us the whole barcode slides slightly towards shorter wavelengths, and when it moves away, towards longer ones. This is the Doppler effect, the same one that drops the pitch of a passing siren, and for speeds far below the speed of light the rule is simple:

$$\frac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda} = \frac{v}{c}$$

In words: the shift of a spectral line, $\Delta\lambda$, as a fraction of its wavelength $\lambda$, equals the star's speed $v$ towards or away from us as a fraction of the speed of light $c$.

Jupiter pulls the Sun round at about 12.5 m/s. Divided by the speed of light, 299,792,458 m/s, that gives a fractional shift of $4.2\times10^{-8}$: a green line at 500 nm moves by 0.000021 nm. That is like measuring a 100-metre football pitch and noticing it has grown by 4 micrometres. No single line can be measured that well, so the spectrographs average thousands of lines and sit in stable rooms away from the telescope.[^queloz2019] HARPS, a spectrograph of the European Southern Observatory (ESO), can detect a star moving at about 1 m/s, a slow walk; its successor ESPRESSO was built to reach a tenth of that.[^esoharps][^esoespresso]

The 12.5 m/s comes from a balance. Star and planet go round their centre of mass in the same time, on opposite sides of it, so the heavier partner moves more slowly in proportion:

$$M_\star v_\star = m_p v_p$$

In words: the star's mass times the star's orbital speed equals the planet's mass times the planet's orbital speed.

Jupiter goes round a circle of radius 5.20 au, or 778 million km, every 11.86 years, which is 13.1 km/s. (An astronomical unit, au, is Earth's average distance from the Sun.) The Sun is 1,048 times heavier, so it moves 1,048 times more slowly: 13,070 ÷ 1,048 = 12.5 m/s. Earth moves at 29.8 km/s, but the Sun outweighs it 333,000 to one, so Earth drags the Sun round at just 9 cm/s. That is just below ESPRESSO's design limit, and the churning surface of a Sun-like star makes more speed noise than that; ESO counts this stellar activity as the main obstacle to finding Earth twins by their wobble.[^esoharps]

Now run the rule backwards. Mayor and Queloz measured a swing of 59 m/s every 4.23 days.[^nobel2019sci] Kepler's third law, $a^3 = M P^2$ with $a$ in au, $P$ in years and $M$ in solar masses, turns the period into a distance: for a star of 1.07 solar masses, $a = 0.052$ au.[^nea2026] Going round a circle of that radius, 7.8 million km, in 4.23 days means moving at 134 km/s. So the planet's mass is the star's mass times 59 ÷ 134,000, which is $1.07 \times 1.99\times10^{30}\ \text{kg} \times 0.00044 = 9.4\times10^{26}$ kg, or 0.49 Jupiter masses. Mayor and Queloz got 0.47 with a slightly different mass for the star.[^nobel2019sci]

There is a catch. A Doppler shift only registers motion along our line of sight, so the wobble gives $m \sin i$, the planet's mass times the sine of the orbit's tilt $i$. That equals the true mass only for an orbit seen edge-on, where $\sin 90° = 1$; for any other tilt the true mass is larger.[^nobel2019sci] For 51 Pegasi b, reflected light picked up in 2015 gave a true mass of about 0.46 Jupiter masses, close to the minimum.[^mayor2019] Other claims were less lucky: a "probable brown dwarf" (too heavy for a planet, too light for a star) around the star HD 114762, announced in 1989, turned out, once Gaia measured its tilt in 2019, to be a small star seen almost face-on.[^latham1989][^kiefer2019]

## Why nobody expected a hot Jupiter

The theory that 51 Pegasi b broke had good physics behind it. Planets form in a disc of gas and dust around a young star. Close to the star it is too warm for water to freeze, so the only solids are rock and metal, and there is little of either. Beyond the snow line, the distance at which water freezes, ice adds a great deal of solid material, and there a core can grow big enough to pull in hydrogen and helium.[^mayor2019] In January 1995, the month Queloz was checking his software, Alan Boss published models in which giant planets form 4 to 5 au from their stars, almost whatever the star's mass.[^boss1995]

51 Pegasi b is 0.052 au from a star 1.37 times as bright as the Sun, which gives it about 500 times Earth's sunlight and a temperature above 1,000 °C.[^nobelpop] It cannot have formed there, so it must have formed farther out and moved in.[^nobel2019sci] The mechanism was already known: in 1980 Peter Goldreich and Scott Tremaine showed that a body embedded in a disc of gas exchanges momentum with it, which can shrink its orbit.[^goldreich1980] By April 1996 Douglas Lin, Peter Bodenheimer and Derek Richardson had shown how 51 Pegasi b could have spiralled in through its disc and stopped close to its star.[^lin1996] As Mayor put it later, this migration had been predicted, but nobody had used the prediction to decide where to look.[^mayor2019]

Giant planets on orbits of a few days, now called hot Jupiters, are rare, found around about 1 in 80 Sun-like stars.[^wright2012] They were found first because they are the easiest planets there are: a big wobble that repeats dozens of times in one observing season. Walker's team, visiting each star a few times a year, had little chance of catching one, as Walker later pointed out.[^walker2008]

## Shadows on a star

A wobble gives a planet's minimum mass but not its size. For that the planet has to pass in front of its star, a transit, which happens only if we see its orbit almost edge-on.

In August 1999 David Latham and Michel Mayor told David Charbonneau about a newly found wobbling star, HD 209458.[^charbonneau2000] Charbonneau and Timothy Brown of the High Altitude Observatory in Boulder, Colorado, had a small transit-hunting camera with a lens about 10 cm across. On 9 and 16 September 1999 the star dimmed for a few hours, just when the orbit said the planet should pass in front of it.[^charbonneau2000] A team led by Greg Henry caught part of a transit independently.[^henry2000] The dip gave the planet's size, 1.27 times Jupiter's radius, and with its mass an average density of about 0.38 grams per cubic centimetre (water is 1), lower than Saturn's.[^charbonneau2000] For the first time a planet around another star had a measured size and density, and it was plainly a gas giant. By late 2001 the same group had seen the star dim slightly more at the yellow wavelength absorbed by sodium: starlight passing through the planet's atmosphere.[^charbonneau2002]

The dip itself is geometry:

$$\delta = \left(\frac{R_p}{R_\star}\right)^2$$

In words: the fraction of the star's light that is blocked, $\delta$, equals the planet's radius divided by the star's radius, squared, because what matters is the area of the planet's disc compared with the area of the star's.

For Jupiter crossing the Sun, 69,911 km ÷ 695,700 km = 0.100, and squaring gives 0.010: a 1% dip. For Earth, 6,371 ÷ 695,700 = 0.0092, and squaring gives 0.000084, or 84 parts per million. HD 209458 b, 1.27 times Jupiter's size in front of a star 1.1 times the Sun's, blocks about 1.3%. Small stars make small planets easy to see: TRAPPIST-1 e is 0.92 times Earth's size, but its star is only 0.12 times the Sun's, so its dip is 0.5%, sixty times deeper than Earth's across the Sun.[^nea2026]

The catch is luck. A planet transits only if we happen to lie in the thin band its shadow sweeps out, and the chance is roughly the star's radius divided by the planet's orbital radius: 1 in 215 for Earth, about 1 in 10 for a hot Jupiter.[^charbonneau2000] To find Earths by their shadows you have to watch a huge number of stars, continuously, for years.

## Kepler's census

William Borucki at NASA's Ames Research Center had been working towards that since 1983. Seeing an Earth cross a Sun-like star means measuring brightness to about 10 parts per million. It took years of experiments to show this could be done, and he proposed the mission five times from 1992 before NASA accepted it in 2001.[^borucki2016]

Kepler was launched on 6 March 2009 and spent four years staring at about 150,000 stars in one patch of sky in Cygnus.[^nasakeplerid][^nasakepler] In 2013 a second reaction wheel, one of the spinning wheels that aimed it, failed, and it carried on as K2, watching a new field every three months or so. NASA retired the spacecraft, out of fuel, on 30 October 2018.[^nasakepler] The NASA Exoplanet Archive credits 2,787 confirmed planets to Kepler and 549 to K2.[^nea2026]

The commonest planet size turned out to be one the Solar System lacks, between Earth and Neptune, and many systems are packed so tightly that our inner Solar System looks empty by comparison.[^nasakepler] Correcting for the planets that do not line up, astronomers could count how common planets really are. Red dwarfs, the most common stars, average about 2.5 planets each between one and four times Earth's size on orbits shorter than 200 days.[^dressing2015] Rocky planets in the habitable zones of Sun-like stars, the distances at which liquid water could last on a planet's surface, number an estimated 0.37 to 0.60 per star, which would put the nearest one around such a star within about 20 light-years of us.[^bryson2021]

Microlensing reaches planets farther from their stars. When one star passes almost exactly in front of a more distant one, its gravity bends and briefly magnifies the background star's light, and a planet around the nearer star adds a blip lasting hours to a couple of days.[^romanexo] A 2012 analysis of six years of microlensing surveys concluded that stars in the Milky Way have, on average, at least one planet each.[^cassan2012]

## Seven worlds and a dim red star

TRAPPIST is a 60-centimetre robotic telescope at ESO's La Silla Observatory in Chile, run from the University of Liège in Belgium, which spends much of its time watching about 60 of the nearest very cool, very dim stars.[^eso1615] In May 2016 Michaël Gillon's team reported three planets around one of them, a red dwarf in Aquarius now called TRAPPIST-1.[^gillon2016] Follow-up with ESO's Very Large Telescope and NASA's Spitzer turned three into seven roughly Earth-sized planets, announced on 22 February 2017.[^gillon2017][^eso1706]

The star has 8% of the Sun's mass, is barely larger than Jupiter and gives out about 0.05% of the Sun's light.[^eso1615] Its outermost planet orbits at a sixth of Mercury's distance from the Sun, and the innermost goes round in a day and a half,[^nea2026] so in scale the system is closer to Jupiter and its moons than to ours (see [Worlds around worlds](#/learn/worlds-around-worlds)).[^eso1615] Going outwards, each planet's period is close to 8/5, 5/3, 3/2, 3/2, 4/3 and 3/2 times the one before, and the planets tug on each other enough to shift the timing of their transits. Eric Agol and colleagues used those shifts to weigh all seven to within 3 to 5%, a precision that would need a spectrograph sensitive to 2.5 cm/s. The densities fit rocky planets a little poorer in iron than Earth.[^agol2021]

The first test of habitability is how much starlight each planet gets:

$$S = \frac{L}{d^2}$$

In words: the starlight a planet receives, $S$, as a multiple of what Earth gets from the Sun, equals the star's luminosity $L$ (its total light output, in units of the Sun's) divided by the square of the planet's distance $d$ from the star in au. It is squared because the light spreads over a sphere whose area grows as the distance squared.

TRAPPIST-1 has $L = 0.00055$. Planet e orbits at 0.0293 au; squared, that is 0.000856, and 0.00055 ÷ 0.000856 = 0.64, so e gets about two-thirds of Earth's sunlight. The same sum gives 1.1 for d, 0.37 for f, 0.25 for g and 4.1 for b, the innermost.[^nea2026] Venus gets 1.9 and Mars 0.43. One widely used climate model puts the Sun's habitable zone between 0.99 and 1.70 au, from about 1.0 down to 0.35 in these units, with the limits moving to somewhat lower light levels around cooler stars;[^kopparapu2013] e, f and g count as inside TRAPPIST-1's.[^eso1706]

The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has been checking whether the planets have atmospheres at all. TRAPPIST-1 b's day side glows about as brightly as a bare rock with no air to carry heat round to the night side.[^greene2023] TRAPPIST-1 c, at 380 K (about 107 °C) on its day side, has no thick carbon-dioxide atmosphere like Venus's.[^zieba2023] For e, four transits observed in 2023 ruled out a thick hydrogen atmosphere but could not tell bare rock from a nitrogen-rich atmosphere like Earth's. Spots on the star contaminate the signal, and more observations are being taken.[^espinoza2025][^glidden2025]

## The nearest planets

Proxima Centauri, a small flaring red dwarf 4.2 light-years away, is the nearest star to the Sun. In early 2016 a team led by Guillem Anglada-Escudé observed it with the HARPS spectrograph on 60 nights, in a public campaign called Pale Red Dot.[^eso1629] On 24 August 2016 they announced that Proxima approaches and retreats at about 5 km/h every 11.2 days: the signature of a planet of at least 1.3 Earth masses, 7 million km from its star.[^eso1629][^anglada2016] ESPRESSO confirmed it in 2020 and in the same data picked up hints of a second planet, announced in 2022 as Proxima d: a quarter of Earth's mass on a 5-day orbit, moving the star at 40 cm/s.[^eso2202] In 2025 the infrared spectrograph NIRPS confirmed d and put b's minimum mass at 1.06 Earth masses; a third claimed planet, c, remains unconfirmed.[^suarez2025]

Proxima is so faint, $L = 0.0015$, that b, at 0.0485 au, gets only 0.64 of Earth's sunlight by the rule above. On light alone it is in the habitable zone. But Proxima's ultraviolet and X-ray flares are far stronger than anything Earth gets from the Sun,[^eso1629] and nobody knows whether a planet that close keeps any air. How long it would take to go and look is the subject of [Rockets to the stars](#/learn/rockets-to-the-stars).

### Barnard's Star, at last

Van de Kamp's star had a second false alarm. In 2018 a large team reported a planet of at least 3.2 Earth masses on a 233-day orbit;[^ribas2018] in 2021 another team traced the signal to the star's own activity, tangled up with the yearly rhythm of when it can be observed.[^lubin2021] Then ESPRESSO took 156 spectra over four years. The 233-day planet was not in them, but a planet of at least 0.37 Earth masses on a 3.15-day orbit was, moving the star at 55 cm/s, with hints of three more.[^gonzalez2024] In March 2025 a team using the MAROON-X spectrograph on the Gemini North telescope confirmed all four, with refined masses. Each has a minimum mass between 0.19 and 0.34 Earth masses and moves the star by less than 50 cm/s, and their data rule out any planet heavier than 0.57 Earth masses in the star's habitable zone.[^basant2025]

So Barnard's Star has planets after all, sixty-two years after van de Kamp said so. Going by their minimum masses, all four are some 1,500 to 2,700 times lighter than the planet he announced. His plates could never have seen them.

## Taking the picture

Photographing a planet beside its star has two problems. The planet is faint: Jupiter reflects about a billionth of the Sun's light, and Earth less still.[^mayor2019] And it is very close to the star on the sky. That angle is easy to work out:

$$\theta = \frac{a}{d}$$

In words: the angle between a planet and its star, in arcseconds, equals the planet's orbital radius in au divided by the star's distance in parsecs. The parsec is defined so that this works; [How far are the stars?](#/learn/how-far-are-the-stars) explains how.

Earth seen from 10 parsecs (32.6 light-years) would be 0.1 arcseconds from the Sun: the width of a human hair seen from about 150 metres, beside a light more than a billion times brighter. Proxima b, 0.0485 au from a star 1.30 parsecs away, sits 0.037 arcseconds from it. Big young planets far from their stars are easier. HR 8799 b is 68 au from a star 41 parsecs away, which puts it 1.65 arcseconds out.[^nea2026]

HR 8799 is a young star, hotter and heavier than the Sun, 134 light-years away in Pegasus.[^nea2026] Its planets still glow with the heat of their formation, which makes them far brighter in infrared light than old planets would be. In November 2008 Christian Marois, Bruce Macintosh and colleagues published infrared images from the Keck and Gemini telescopes showing three planets at 24, 38 and 68 au, each 5 to 13 times Jupiter's mass, all seen moving the same way round the star in images taken years apart.[^marois2008] A fourth followed in 2010.[^marois2010] They had been in older data all along: re-analysis found two of them in 2004 images, and a new way of subtracting starlight found the outermost in Hubble images from 1998.[^lafreniere2009]

The same issue of Science carried Paul Kalas's Hubble images of a dot beside Fomalhaut, 25 light-years away.[^kalas2008] Fomalhaut b never behaved like a planet: it was bright in visible light, missing in the infrared, and faded and spread out over the years. In 2020 András Gáspár and George Rieke concluded that it was most likely an expanding dust cloud from a collision.[^gaspar2020]

## Reading the air of other worlds

When a planet crosses its star, a thin ring of its atmosphere is lit from behind. Gases in that ring absorb at their own wavelengths, so at those wavelengths the planet looks slightly bigger and the dip slightly deeper. Measure the depth of the dip colour by colour and you have a spectrum of the planet's air. The effect is a few hundredths of a per cent of the starlight even for a hot, puffed-up giant, and far less for a small rocky planet.

WASP-39 b is such a giant: about Saturn's mass, 1.3 times Jupiter's width and around 900 °C, 700 light-years away. On 10 July 2022 JWST watched it transit, and between 4.1 and 4.6 micrometres its silhouette swelled: the first clear detection of carbon dioxide in a planet beyond the Solar System.[^nasawasp39][^ers2023]

Small, temperate planets are far harder, as K2-18 b shows. It has 8.6 times Earth's mass and 2.6 times its radius and orbits in the habitable zone of a red dwarf 124 light-years away in Leo.[^cambridge2025] It is a sub-Neptune, the in-between kind of planet Kepler found so common, and what such planets are made of is still argued over.[^nasak218] Hubble data in 2019 seemed to show water vapour,[^tsiaras2019] which a 2022 re-analysis attributed to methane instead.[^bezard2022] In September 2023 Nikku Madhusudhan's group at Cambridge reported JWST spectra with methane and carbon dioxide, about 1% each, in a hydrogen-rich atmosphere. They argued that this fitted a "Hycean" world, an ocean planet under a hydrogen sky, and noted a weak hint of dimethyl sulfide (DMS), a gas made on Earth by living things, mostly marine plankton.[^madhusudhan2023][^nasak218]

In April 2025 the group reported mid-infrared data which, they said, DMS or its relative dimethyl disulfide could explain and most other molecules expected on K2-18 b could not. They put the evidence at three sigma. Cambridge's press release called it the strongest hint yet of biological activity outside the Solar System, while noting that three sigma means about a 0.3% chance of a fluke, against the five sigma normally needed for a discovery.[^madhusudhan2025][^cambridge2025] That 0.3% assumes the model is right. If the model leaves out a molecule, or the data processing has a small error, the real chance of a fluke is much larger, and that is where the argument went.

Stephen Schmidt's team reprocessed the 2023 spectra with 60 data treatments and more than 250 model fits and found methane but no reliable carbon dioxide or DMS.[^schmidt2025] Rafael Luque and colleagues fitted all the data together, from 0.6 to 12 micrometres, and found that other molecules, such as ethane, fit just as well; the preference for DMS came from limiting which molecules the models could use.[^luque2025] In July 2025 Renyu Hu's team added four new JWST transits, in which the DMS signal was marginal, below two sigma unless an unusual haze was assumed.[^hu2025] DMS has also turned up in a comet and in a gas cloud near the centre of the Milky Way, places without plankton, and models show it can form without life in a hydrogen-rich atmosphere.[^sanznovo2025][^hu2025] A 2026 study concluded that the spectrum is best read as that of a sub-Neptune, with no need for DMS at all.[^tsai2026]

::: myth JWST has found signs of life on K2-18 b.
It has found methane. The one tentative DMS signal did not hold up in independent analyses or new transits, and DMS turns up in a comet and in interstellar gas, where no living thing made it.[^luque2025][^hu2025][^sanznovo2025]
:::

## What comes next

### Gaia's fourth data release

Gaia, a satellite of the European Space Agency (ESA), maps the positions of stars across the whole sky. Its fourth data release, from 66 months of observations, will for the first time publish every individual position measurement: the raw material for spotting the kind of wobble van de Kamp was after, at a precision his plates could never reach.[^gaiadr4] NASA expects thousands of new planet candidates from it.[^nasa6000]

**Status:** scheduled for 2 December 2026.[^gaiadr4]

### PLATO

ESA's PLATO carries 26 cameras and will watch more than 200,000 stars for transits of rocky planets out to the habitable zones of Sun-like stars. It must pick out dips smaller than 80 parts per million, just below the 84 that Earth makes crossing the Sun.[^esaplato][^esaplato2026]

**Status:** built and tested. Launch on an Ariane 6 was planned for the end of 2026, then January 2027; ESA's mission page now says March 2027, so treat the month as uncertain.[^esaplato][^esaplato2026]

### The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope

Roman, delivered ahead of schedule and on budget, launched on a Falcon Heavy on 30 August 2026.[^nasaroman] It will watch 200 million stars towards the centre of the Milky Way (see [Our galaxy](#/learn/our-galaxy)) for microlensing, which should find more than a thousand planets from the habitable zone outwards, including planets drifting free of any star, and perhaps 100,000 more by transits. Its coronagraph, an instrument that blocks out a star's light, will try to photograph Jupiter-like planets in reflected light.[^romanexo]

**Status:** being switched on and tested. Its main camera and coronagraph came on in September 2026, and NASA expects the first images by early 2027.[^romanblog][^nasaroman]

### JWST's Rocky Worlds programme

A 500-hour JWST programme is taking the day-side temperatures of rocky planets around red dwarfs, the test used on TRAPPIST-1 b and c, while Hubble measures the stars' ultraviolet light.[^rockyworlds]

**Status:** under way since 2025.[^rockyworlds]

### The Extremely Large Telescope

ESO's ELT in Chile will have a main mirror 39 metres across, made of 798 segments,[^elttimeline] and ESO expects it to image planets in the habitable zones of some nearby stars.[^eltexo] Its sharpest resolution, $1.22\lambda/D$ for wavelength $\lambda$ and mirror width $D$, is about 0.0065 arcseconds at 1 micrometre: enough, in principle, to separate Proxima b from its star.

**Status:** under construction. First light is planned for 2029 and the first science observations for December 2030.[^elttimeline]

### Ariel

ESA's Ariel will survey the atmospheres of about 1,000 known planets.[^esaariel]

**Status:** in development; launch planned for 2031.[^esaariel]

### The Habitable Worlds Observatory

NASA's next flagship astrophysics mission after Roman is meant to directly image about 25 potentially habitable planets and look for signs of life in their light.[^nasahwo] The 2021 decadal survey, US astronomy's ten-year plan, recommended a telescope of about 6 metres, estimated to cost 11 billion dollars.[^astro2020]

**Status:** concept and technology studies. No launch date has been set; the usual expectation is the 2040s, and both the date and the money are uncertain.[^payload2024]

### Open questions

Nobody yet knows whether rocky planets around red dwarfs keep their atmospheres, what sub-Neptunes are made of, or how many Sun-like stars have rocky planets in their habitable zones: Kepler's answer is uncertain by more than a factor of two.[^bryson2021] K2-18 b has also shown that astronomers have not yet agreed what evidence would be enough to claim life on another planet.
