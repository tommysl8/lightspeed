On 4 February 1917 Albert Einstein, in wartime Berlin, wrote to his friend Paul Ehrenfest in Leiden. He had just done something in gravitation theory, he said, that exposed him "a bit to the danger of being committed to a madhouse".[^oraif2017] Four days later he read the paper to the Prussian Academy of Sciences. It applied his general theory of relativity, barely a year old, to the whole universe at once.[^oraif2017]

Everyone knew what the universe was like. Stars drift about, but on the whole nothing goes anywhere, and Einstein built that into his calculation as an assumption.[^oraif2018] His equations would not cooperate. A universe full of matter pulls on itself and cannot simply hang there, so he added one extra term: a constant, written with the Greek letter lambda, Λ, that pushes gently outwards over enormous distances and holds everything in balance.[^oraif2018]

He dropped it fourteen years later, and in 1998 it came back. In between, a Russian meteorologist and a Belgian priest worked out that the universe ought to be expanding, astronomers in California found that it is, and two radio astronomers at a telephone company's laboratory in New Jersey picked up the leftover heat of its hot beginning in an antenna that pigeons had been roosting in.

## A universe that would not stand still

Einstein's static universe was finely balanced. The amount of matter fixed the size of space, which curved round on itself like the surface of a globe, and it fixed the value of Λ too.[^oraif2018] Within months the Dutch astronomer Willem de Sitter found a second solution of the same equations: a universe with no matter in it at all, in which light from distant objects would arrive reddened.[^aipexp] Einstein disliked it. He admitted to de Sitter in April 1917 that observations would one day settle whether Λ was really there, adding: "Conviction is a good mainspring, but a bad judge!"[^oraif2018]

The first to take the equations at their word was Alexander Friedmann, a mathematician and meteorologist at the Main Geophysical Observatory in Petrograd. His paper "On the curvature of space" reached the *Zeitschrift für Physik* on 29 June 1922. It showed that Einstein's equations also allow universes whose size changes with time. Some grow for ever; others grow and shrink in cycles.[^mactutor][^friedmann1922]

Einstein's reply reached the journal on 18 September. Friedmann's solution, he wrote, did not satisfy the field equations. Friedmann posted him the calculation on 6 December, by which time Einstein had left for Japan. Only in May 1923, when Friedmann's colleague Krutkov caught up with him at Ehrenfest's house in Leiden and went through it, did Einstein admit that his objection rested on an error in his own calculation, and say so in print.[^mactutor] An unpublished draft of that retraction added that a physical meaning could hardly be ascribed to Friedmann's universe.[^oraif2018]

Friedmann did not live to see anyone take it seriously. In July 1925 he rode a balloon to a record 7,400 m to make weather and medical observations. A few weeks later he fell ill with typhoid, and he died on 16 September 1925, aged 37.[^mactutor]

## The priest who measured it first

On the night of 17 September 1912, at the Lowell Observatory in Flagstaff, Arizona, Vesto Slipher photographed the spectrum of the Andromeda Nebula. Its dark lines, each set by a particular element, were shifted towards the blue. That is the Doppler effect, which also raises the pitch of an approaching siren, and further exposures that December showed Andromeda coming towards us at about 300 km/s, so fast that some astronomers refused to believe it.[^aipexp] By 1917 he had measured 25 spiral nebulae, and 21 of them were moving away, their lines shifted towards the red: a redshift.[^luminet2013] How far away they were stayed unknown until Edwin Hubble found Cepheids in them in the mid-1920s, pulsing stars whose rhythm gives away their true brightness; [Island universes](#/learn/island-universes) tells that story.

Georges Lemaître was ordained a Catholic priest in 1923 and spent the next two years studying with Arthur Eddington in Cambridge and then at Harvard and MIT.[^luminet2013] Back in Belgium in 1927 he published a paper in the *Annales de la Société scientifique de Bruxelles* whose title gave away the plot: "A homogeneous universe of constant mass and increasing radius accounting for the radial velocity of extra-galactic nebulae".[^luminet2013]

Lemaître found the expanding solutions of Einstein's equations for himself, not knowing Friedmann had got there first, and he said the redshifts are not galaxies flying through space but space itself stretching the light as it travels.[^luminet2013] Then he checked it against the sky. He took 42 galaxies, with speeds from a list compiled by Gustav Strömberg, most of them Slipher's measurements, and distances estimated from their brightness with a formula of Hubble's. Speed rose in proportion to distance: 625 km/s for every megaparsec, or 575 without the statistical weighting he preferred.[^luminet2013] A megaparsec (Mpc) is 3.26 million light-years.

That October, at the Solvay Conference in Brussels, Lemaître walked with Einstein in the Parc Léopold. Einstein had read the paper. He told Lemaître the calculations were correct but the physics was abominable, and pointed him to Friedmann.[^luminet2013] Eddington, sent a copy by his former student, forgot about it.

In January 1930 Eddington and de Sitter puzzled over the galaxy velocities in public, at a meeting of the Royal Astronomical Society in London. Lemaître wrote to remind Eddington that he had solved the problem three years earlier. Eddington, embarrassed, spread the word, showed that Einstein's static universe was unstable, like a pen balanced on its point, and arranged for the paper to appear in English in the society's *Monthly Notices*.[^luminet2013]

The translation came out in March 1931. The paragraphs with the data, the proportionality and the 625 had been replaced by one sentence that simply adopted a number.[^luminet2013][^lemaitre1931] In 2011 two astronomers suggested the paper had been censored to protect Hubble's priority. Later that year the astrophysicist Mario Livio found the answer in the archives. Lemaître had translated the paper himself, and on 9 March 1931 he told the editor, William Smart, that he had left out "the provisional discussion of radial velocities which is clearly of no actual interest".[^livio2011][^luminet2013] By then Hubble had far better data, and Lemaître had moved on to a bigger idea. In 1950 he remarked that the title of his 1927 paper left no doubt about what he had meant.[^vdb2011]

::: myth Edwin Hubble discovered that the universe is expanding.
Lemaître derived the expansion from general relativity and measured its rate in 1927, two years before Hubble's paper, which used much the same data.[^luminet2013][^vdb2011] Hubble nailed down the straight-line relation but stayed sceptical of an expanding universe all his life.[^luminet2013] In 2018 the International Astronomical Union's members voted, 78% in favour, to recommend calling it the Hubble–Lemaître law.[^iau2018]
:::

## Hubble, Humason and a straight line

In July 1928 the International Astronomical Union met in Leiden, and Hubble talked cosmology with de Sitter.[^iau1928][^aipexp] Back at Mount Wilson in California he asked his observing assistant, Milton Humason, to find out whether fainter, presumably more distant, galaxies had larger redshifts.[^vdb2011][^aipexp] Humason measured new velocities; Hubble worked out the distances.[^aipexp]

Hubble's six-page paper appeared in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* on 15 March 1929. Most of its velocities were still Slipher's, and Slipher went uncredited.[^luminet2013] For 24 galaxies with individual distances he got 465 ± 50 km/s per megaparsec, and grouping them gave 513 ± 60. Only in the last paragraph did he mention theory, and then only to say the relation might be the effect de Sitter's model predicted.[^hubble1929][^luminet2013] Two years later Hubble and Humason added 40 new velocities, out to a cluster of galaxies in Leo receding at 19,600 km/s, and the straight line held.[^hh1931][^vdb2011]

Einstein visited California early in 1931, and in April he threw Λ out. Hubble had shown the universe was not static, and Einstein now accepted that his solution was unstable too: "On these grounds alone, I am no longer inclined to ascribe a physical meaning to my former solution."[^oraif2018] In 1932 he and de Sitter proposed the simplest expanding universe of all, flat and without Λ.[^eds1932]

### Redshift is stretching

A redshift moves every line in a galaxy's spectrum to a longer wavelength. Astronomers sum it up in one number, $z$:

$$1 + z = \frac{\lambda_{\text{seen}}}{\lambda_{\text{sent}}}$$

In words: one plus the redshift equals the wavelength we see divided by the wavelength the galaxy sent out.

Hydrogen glows at 656.3 nm (a nanometre is a billionth of a metre); from the Coma Cluster of galaxies the same line arrives at 671.66 nm.[^simbad] Then $1 + z = 671.66 \div 656.3 = 1.0234$, so $z = 0.0234$. For small redshifts the speed is close to $z$ times the speed of light: $0.0234 \times 299{,}792 = 7{,}015$ km/s, so Coma is receding at about 7,000 km/s.

Lemaître's picture gives $1 + z$ a second meaning: it is the number of times the universe has stretched while the light was on its way. For JADES-GS-z14-0, one of the most distant galaxies known, the ALMA radio telescopes pinned the redshift at 14.18, so the universe has grown 15.18 times since that light set out.[^carniani2025] Ultraviolet light the galaxy gave out at 121.6 nm now arrives in the infrared at 1,846 nm, which is why it took the James Webb Space Telescope to find it. Treating a shift that large as an ordinary Doppler shift gives wrong answers, and measurements of distant supernovae rule that reading out by 23 standard deviations: 23 times the typical random scatter of the data.[^davis2004]

### The Hubble–Lemaître law

Lemaître's straight line, and Hubble's, says that twice as far means twice as fast. The number linking the two is the Hubble constant, $H_0$: the extra speed, in km/s, that each megaparsec of distance adds.

$$v = H_0\, d$$

In words: a galaxy's speed away from us equals the Hubble constant times its distance.

Planck, the European satellite that mapped the Big Bang's afterglow, puts $H_0$ at 67.4 km/s per megaparsec.[^planck2018] The Virgo Cluster, the nearest big cluster of galaxies, is 16.5 Mpc away, or 53.8 million light-years.[^mei2007] The law predicts $67.4 \times 16.5 = 1{,}112$ km/s, and the cluster's measured speed is about 1,140 km/s.[^simbad] They agree within the uncertainty in the distance. Galaxies also swing about under each other's gravity at hundreds of kilometres per second, which is why the 1927 and 1929 data, all from nearby galaxies, scattered so badly.[^mei2007][^luminet2013] Closer in, gravity wins outright. The expansion would carry Andromeda, 2.5 million light-years away, off at 52 km/s; instead it is falling towards the Milky Way at 109 km/s.[^nasam31][^vdmarel2012]

## Running the film backwards

If every galaxy is moving away at a speed proportional to its distance, run time backwards and they all meet. A galaxy at distance $d$ moving at $v = H_0 d$ would take $d \div v = 1/H_0$ to cover that distance, whatever $d$ is. That time has a name:

$$t_H = \frac{1}{H_0}$$

In words: the Hubble time is one divided by the Hubble constant.

The units need care. $H_0$ is 67.4 km/s per megaparsec, and a megaparsec is $3.086 \times 10^{19}$ km, so $1/H_0 = 3.086 \times 10^{19} \div 67.4 = 4.58 \times 10^{17}$ s. A year is $3.156 \times 10^{7}$ s, so the Hubble time is 14.5 billion years. With Hubble's 500 km/s per megaparsec the same sum gives 1.96 billion years, and in the Einstein–de Sitter universe, which slows down as it goes, the age is two-thirds of that: 1.3 billion years.[^oraif2018]

That was a disaster. Geologists dating rocks by their radioactivity already had the Earth older than that.[^aipdouble] The rescue came in the 1950s, when Walter Baade showed that there are two kinds of Cepheid star and Hubble had calibrated his distances with the wrong kind. Distances to galaxies doubled, and by the end of the decade Allan Sandage had $H_0$ down to 75 ± 25.[^aipdouble][^oraif2018] Planck now puts the age of the universe at 13.797 ± 0.023 billion years.[^planck2018] That is close to the Hubble time but not equal, because the expansion has not run steadily: it slowed for most of history and, with Planck's numbers, has been speeding up for about the last six billion years.

## What is actually expanding

Eddington explained it in 1931 with a toy: picture the galaxies "embedded in the surface of a rubber balloon which is being inflated".[^kragh2013] Blow up the balloon and every dot moves away from every other dot, faster the further apart they are, and no dot is the centre. A loaf of raisin bread rising in the oven does the same in three dimensions. Two raisins 1 cm apart end up 2 cm apart; two that started 2 cm apart end up 4 cm apart. The far pair moved twice as fast, which is the Hubble–Lemaître law in a kitchen.

Both pictures break if you push them. A balloon has an inside and an outside, and a loaf has a crust. As far as anyone can tell, the universe has no edge, no centre and nothing outside it to expand into. The raisins get one thing right: they do not swell up themselves.

::: myth The Big Bang was an explosion at one point in space, and the galaxies are flying out from it.
There is no centre and no edge. Observers in every galaxy see the others moving away just as we do, and redshifts are stretched light, not the Doppler shifts of debris flying through space; real supernova data rule that reading out.[^davis2004]
:::

::: myth Everything is expanding, including atoms, people and the Solar System.
Things held together by their own forces do not take part. Atoms are held by electric forces, the Earth's orbit by the Sun's gravity, and galaxies and clusters by gravity too; for all of them the effect of cosmic expansion is insignificant.[^cooperstock1998] If the Earth's orbit did share in the expansion, it would widen by about 10 m a year.
:::

### How far is far?

Expansion makes "distance" slippery. With Planck's numbers, the light from JADES-GS-z14-0 set out 13.5 billion years ago, when the galaxy was 2.2 billion light-years from the matter that would become the Milky Way. Today it is 33.7 billion light-years away and receding at 2.3 times the speed of light.[^planck2018] Nothing is broken. Relativity forbids anything from overtaking a beam of light where it is ([Why nothing outruns light](#/learn/nothing-outruns-light) has the rule); it sets no limit on how fast the space between two distant places can grow, and we see plenty of galaxies that are receding faster than light.[^davis2004] [The edge of reach](#/learn/the-edge-of-reach) works through these distances and the limit they imply: because the expansion is speeding up, there is a distance beyond which no signal sent today, and so no ship, can ever arrive.

## A hot beginning

Eddington found the idea of a beginning repugnant. Lemaître answered him in *Nature* on 9 May 1931: go back far enough in time and all the energy in the universe must have been packed into a few quanta, or even one.[^lemaitre1931n] He called it the primeval atom.[^luminet2013]

George Gamow, a Ukrainian-born physicist working in the United States, took the idea into nuclear physics. From 1946 he asked what a young universe hot and dense enough to be a nuclear furnace would have cooked.[^aipbigbang] His student Ralph Alpher did the calculations for his thesis. When they wrote them up, Gamow added the name of Hans Bethe, who had done none of the work, so that the authors would read Alpher, Bethe, Gamow: alpha, beta, gamma. The paper appeared on 1 April 1948.[^abg1948][^aipgamow] Bethe said later that he "felt at the time that it was rather a nice joke".[^aipgamow] Gamow also urged Alpher's colleague Robert Herman to change his name to Delter. Herman declined.[^aipgamow]

Building every element by adding neutrons one at a time did not work, because no stable nucleus has 5 or 8 particles and the chain stalls after helium.[^pdg2024] Gamow joked that the theory still accounted for 99% of the matter in the universe.[^aipbigbang] He was nearer the truth than the joke suggested. The heavier elements came later, from stars ([What stars are made of](#/learn/what-stars-are-made-of) tells how), but almost all the helium was made in the first few minutes.

### Three minutes of cooking

In its first second the universe was hotter than ten billion degrees, a soup of protons, neutrons, electrons, neutrinos and light in which protons and neutrons kept turning into each other. Neutrons are slightly heavier, so they were always a little outnumbered. As the universe cooled, the swapping became too slow to keep up, and the ratio froze at about one neutron for every six protons. Free neutrons decay, with an average lifetime of about 878 seconds,[^pdgn] so by the time it was cool enough for nuclei to survive, below about a billion degrees, the ratio had fallen to about one in seven. Almost every remaining neutron was then locked into helium-4, and by about three minutes the recipe was fixed, in gas about as dense as air.[^pdg2024]

That fixes the helium share by counting. Each helium nucleus uses two neutrons, so the fraction of all the mass that ends up as helium is

$$Y = \frac{2\,(n/p)}{1 + n/p}$$

In words: the helium fraction by mass is twice the neutron-to-proton ratio, divided by one plus that ratio.

With one neutron for every seven protons, $n/p = 1/7$ and $Y = (2/7) \div (8/7) = 0.25$. Check it with sixteen particles: 2 neutrons and 14 protons. The 2 neutrons pair up with 2 protons to make one helium nucleus of mass 4, leaving 12 lone protons, which are hydrogen. Helium is 4 of the 16 units of mass, or 25%. Measurements of glowing gas in small galaxies with almost no heavy elements give 24.5% ± 0.3%.[^pdg2024] Even Fred Hoyle, no friend of a hot beginning, worked out in 1964 that stars could not have made that much helium.[^penzias1978]

The calculations said something else too. The light that filled the early universe should still be here, stretched and cooled. In November 1948 Alpher and Herman put its temperature today at about 5 degrees above absolute zero.[^ah1948][^wilson1978] Nobody went looking; Gamow thought so faint a glow could not be told apart from starlight.[^penzias1978]

## Big Bang or steady state

In 1948 three friends from wartime radar work in Britain, Hermann Bondi, Thomas Gold and Fred Hoyle, proposed a different universe. It expands, but it looks the same at every place and at every time: as galaxies move apart, new matter appears in the gaps and new galaxies form from it. Bondi and Gold's paper appeared first, Hoyle's a few months later.[^bondigold1948][^hoyle1948] The rate of creation needed was tiny, by Hoyle's reckoning about one atom a year in a volume the size of St Paul's Cathedral.[^kragh2013] Einstein had sketched a similar model, probably in early 1931, and seems to have dropped it after finding a mistake.[^oraif2014]

It had real attractions: no beginning to explain, and no age problem. On 28 March 1949 Hoyle gave a twenty-minute talk on the BBC's Third Programme. Describing the rival theories, he said they assumed "all the matter of the universe was created in one big bang at a particular time in the remote past".[^kragh2013] He thought the idea unscientific and said so. The name stuck. In 1993 *Sky & Telescope* ran a competition to replace it; it drew 13,099 entries, and the judges, Carl Sagan among them, picked none.[^kragh2013]

::: myth Fred Hoyle coined "Big Bang" to mock the theory.
Hoyle said he was reaching for vivid pictures on radio and did not mean it as an insult, and there is no record that Gamow, Lemaître or anyone else took offence at the time. The often-told BBC debate in which Hoyle sneered at Gamow never happened.[^kragh2013]
:::

The steady state made a sharp prediction. Looking far away means looking back in time, and in a universe that never changes the distant past should look like today. In the early 1960s counts of distant radio sources and the discovery of quasars, the blazing cores of very distant galaxies, suggested it did not: the universe had changed.[^kragh2013] Then came a noise in New Jersey.

## The noise in the horn

On Crawford Hill in Holmdel, New Jersey, Bell Telephone Laboratories had an odd antenna: a 20-foot (6 m) horn, a giant flared funnel with a curved reflector at its mouth, built in 1960 to catch signals bounced off the Echo balloon satellite. In 1963 Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, two young radio astronomers, took it over for research, because every scrap of noise it picked up could be accounted for.[^wilson1978]

Radio astronomers measure faint signals as temperatures: the temperature a warm object would need to give off the same signal. Wilson wanted to measure a faint radio glow thought to surround our galaxy. As a first check that the system worked, they pointed the horn away from the Milky Way at a wavelength of 7.35 cm, where that glow should have been under 0.1 K, and expected almost nothing.[^wilson1978] Instead there was about 3.5 K too much, in every direction, and it did not change from season to season. They ruled out the atmosphere, New York City, the Galaxy and every known radio source, and suspected the antenna. A pair of pigeons had been roosting in the narrow end of the horn and had coated it with what Wilson later called "a white material familiar to all city dwellers".[^wilson1978] They evicted the birds and cleaned up, and the noise barely changed. In spring 1965 they taped over the riveted joints. Still there.[^wilson1978]

The answer came by telephone. Penzias mentioned the noise to Bernard Burke of MIT, who had heard about work by Jim Peebles, a young theorist in Robert Dicke's group at Princeton. Peebles had calculated that the universe should be filled with radiation at no less than 10 K, and Dicke's team was building a receiver to look for it. The Princeton group visited Holmdel and was quickly convinced, and in July 1965 the two groups published side-by-side letters in the *Astrophysical Journal*, Princeton's explaining the radiation and Bell Labs' simply reporting it.[^wilson1978][^pw1965][^dicke1965]

The radiation had been hiding in plain sight. In 1941 Andrew McKellar, in Canada, found that cyanogen molecules in interstellar space behave as if bathed in radiation at 2.3 K.[^wilson1978] Bell Labs' own 1961 report on the Echo receiver showed an unexplained excess of about 3 K, and in 1964 two Soviet theorists, Doroshkevich and Novikov, named the Holmdel horn as the best instrument for finding the relic radiation, then misread the Echo report and decided it wasn't there.[^penzias1978] Penzias and Wilson shared half of the 1978 Nobel Prize in Physics.[^nobel1978]

::: myth Penzias and Wilson were searching for the afterglow of the Big Bang.
They were checking a radio telescope and expected to find nothing. They also left all cosmology out of their paper on purpose, because they had not worked on the theory.[^wilson1978]
:::

### Why it glows like a blackbody

For its first 380,000 years or so the universe was a hot plasma of nuclei and free electrons, and light could not travel far before bouncing off an electron.[^esa2013] Matter and light kept trading energy until the light had the spectrum of a perfect blackbody, the shape of glow that anything takes on when everything is at one temperature. When the temperature fell to about 3,000 K, electrons settled onto nuclei, the fog cleared, and the light has flown freely ever since. That light is the cosmic microwave background (CMB).

Expansion stretches every wavelength by the same factor, $1 + z$. A stretched blackbody is still a blackbody, only cooler:

$$T = T_0\,(1 + z)$$

In words: the temperature of the background radiation at redshift $z$ equals its temperature today times one plus $z$.

Today $T_0 = 2.7255$ K.[^fixsen2009] The light was released at a redshift of about 1,090, so it started out at $2.7255 \times 1{,}091 = 2{,}970$ K.[^planck2018] Wien's law puts the peak of a blackbody at 2.898 mm divided by the temperature in kelvin: about 1 micrometre at release, in the near infrared, and 1.06 mm now, in the microwaves.

In 2022 astronomers tested the formula 12.8 billion years back, with water vapour in the galaxy HFLS3, at a redshift of 6.34, absorbing the background radiation behind it. The formula predicts $2.7255 \times 7.34 = 20.0$ K there; the water implied between 16.4 and 30.2 K.[^riechers2022] A fast enough ship does the reverse, blueshifting the glow ahead of it into visible light; see [What you would see near the speed of light](#/learn/seeing-near-light-speed).

## A perfect curve

COBE, NASA's Cosmic Background Explorer, was proposed in 1974 to fly on a Delta rocket and later designed around the Space Shuttle. After Challenger was lost in January 1986 it was redesigned for a Delta again, and it launched from Vandenberg in California on 18 November 1989. Alpher and Herman came to watch.[^mather2006]

In January 1990 John Mather showed COBE's first spectrum at the American Astronomical Society meeting outside Washington. The measurements sat on the blackbody curve, and an audience of more than a thousand rose in a standing ovation.[^mather2006] The final analysis gave a blackbody at 2.725 K, with the measurements straying from the curve by only 50 parts per million of its peak, on average.[^mather2006]

Another of COBE's instruments looked for tiny differences in temperature between directions. On 23 April 1992 George Smoot's team announced them: ripples of about one part in 100,000.[^smoot2006] Slightly denser patches pulled in more matter over billions of years, and those ripples grew into galaxies. Mather and Smoot shared the 2006 Nobel Prize in Physics.[^nobel2006]

NASA's WMAP mapped the ripples in finer detail from 2001 to 2010, and ESA's Planck, launched on 14 May 2009, mapped them in nine frequency bands until 2013.[^wmap][^planck2018i] Planck's final 2018 results fitted over a billion map pixels with a standard model described by just six numbers, built from ordinary matter, Λ, and dark matter, which pulls by gravity but gives off no light.[^planck2018i] The map also shows the Sun moving at 370 km/s relative to the background, which makes the sky slightly warmer ahead of us and cooler behind.[^planck2018i]

## Speeding up

By the 1990s most astronomers assumed gravity was slowing the expansion down; the question was by how much, and whether it would ever stop. Two teams set out to measure it with Type Ia supernovae. Each is the explosion of a white dwarf, a star as heavy as the Sun packed into the size of the Earth, briefly as bright as a whole galaxy.[^nobel2011] After a correction based on how quickly each one fades, they are almost equally bright, so how faint one looks tells you how far away it is.[^perlmutter1999] The Supernova Cosmology Project, led by Saul Perlmutter at Berkeley, started in 1988. The High-z Supernova Search Team was led by Brian Schmidt in Australia, with Adam Riess as lead author of its key paper.[^nobel2011][^riess1998]

In 1998 both teams got the same shock. The distant supernovae were too faint. The High-z team's 16 distant supernovae were on average 10 to 15% further away than they should have been in a thinly filled universe without Λ.[^riess1998] Brightness falls as one over distance squared, so 10% further means $1/1.10^2 = 0.83$ of the expected brightness and 15% further means $1/1.15^2 = 0.76$: about a fifth dimmer. Perlmutter's team, with 42 supernovae, found the same.[^perlmutter1999] The expansion is not slowing down. It is speeding up. In 2011 half the Nobel Prize went to Perlmutter and half to Schmidt and Riess.[^nobel2011]

Whatever does the pushing is called dark energy. The simplest version is Einstein's Λ: an energy that belongs to empty space itself, the same in every cubic metre, so that as space grows there is more of it. With Planck's numbers it makes up 68.5% of everything today.[^planck2018] Nobody knows what it is. When Yakov Zel'dovich estimated the energy of empty space from quantum theory in 1968, he got an answer too big by a factor of more than $10^{40}$, a mismatch now called the cosmological constant problem.[^oraif2018]

::: myth Einstein called the cosmological constant his "biggest blunder".
The phrase comes from George Gamow, who said Einstein told him so. Nothing Einstein wrote says it, and historians disagree about how far Gamow's memory can be trusted.[^oraif2018]
:::

## Two rulers that disagree

### The Hubble tension

There are two ways to get $H_0$. One starts from the early universe: take the Planck map, assume the standard model, and work out how fast a universe like that should be expanding today. That gives 67.4 ± 0.5 km/s per Mpc.[^planck2018] Adding maps from the South Pole Telescope and the Atacama Cosmology Telescope gives 67.19 ± 0.38.[^camphuis2026]

The other is a ladder of distances ([How far are the stars?](#/learn/how-far-are-the-stars) has the bottom rungs). Parallax fixes the distances of Cepheid stars in the Milky Way, Cepheids give the distances of nearby galaxies that have hosted Type Ia supernovae, and those supernovae calibrate the ones far out. In 2022 the SH0ES team, led by Riess, used Cepheids in the host galaxies of 42 supernovae to get 73.04 ± 1.04, five standard deviations from Planck.[^riess2022] In 2025 a large group of experts pooling every good local method got 73.50 ± 0.81.[^h0dn2026] Wendy Freedman's Chicago–Carnegie team, using the brightest red giant stars instead of Cepheids, gets 70.4 with an uncertainty of about 2, between the two camps.[^freedman2025]

The gap is 8 or 9%, and it shows up close to home. Twelve Type Ia supernovae have gone off in the Coma Cluster, and the local ladder puts it 98.5 ± 2.2 Mpc away. Calibrated to Planck's expansion rate instead, DESI's survey of Coma's galaxies would put it at 111.8 Mpc.[^scolnic2025]

::: see-it go:coma-cluster
The Coma Cluster is about 320 million light-years away by the local ladder; Planck's expansion rate would put it more than 10% further.
:::

Either one side has a hidden error or the standard model is missing something, perhaps in the first few hundred thousand years. As of September 2026 nobody knows which.

### A ruler made of sound

In the hot plasma before the background radiation was released, pressure waves rippled outwards from every denser clump, the way sound travels through air. When the plasma turned into neutral gas the waves froze, leaving a slight preference for lumps of matter to sit 147 Mpc apart, about 480 million light-years.[^planck2018] Galaxies later formed along that pattern, called baryon acoustic oscillations. It is a ruler of known length printed across the sky, and how big it looks at different redshifts shows how the universe expanded.[^desi2025]

The Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument, DESI, measures it with 5,000 robot-positioned optical fibres on the 4-metre Mayall Telescope at Kitt Peak in Arizona, each catching the spectrum of a different galaxy. Its first results came out on 4 April 2024.[^desi2024] Its three-year results, released on 19 March 2025 and based on more than 14 million galaxies and quasars, fitted the standard model on their own. Combined with the background radiation and supernovae, though, they preferred dark energy that is getting weaker with time, by 2.8 to 4.2 standard deviations depending on which supernovae were used.[^desidr2] Physicists want five before claiming a discovery; a three-sigma result turns up by chance about 0.3% of the time, and plenty have faded. "We're guided by Occam's razor, and the simplest explanation for what we see is shifting," said Will Percival, one of DESI's two co-spokespeople.[^desi2025]

In July 2026 DESI's new analysis of hydrogen gas in front of distant quasars came out closer to a constant Λ, and the team wrote that the hints might fade away.[^desi2026b] The full five-year survey, with more than 47 million galaxies and quasars, should settle much of it.[^desi2026a]

:::

## What comes next

### DESI's full map

**Status:** the planned five-year survey was completed in April 2026, and the instrument keeps observing through 2028, growing its map from 14,000 to 17,000 square degrees. The first dark-energy results from the full survey are expected in 2027.[^desi2026a]

### Euclid

**Status:** in space since 1 July 2023, mapping galaxies in three dimensions across a third of the sky over six years.[^euclid2025] ESA had promised the first cosmology data for October 2026, but in June 2026 it split the release in two: images, catalogues and spectra covering about 1,900 square degrees in November 2026, and the complete release, with the products needed for cosmology, in mid-2027. The first cosmology results are now expected in 2027; treat both dates as provisional.[^euclid2025][^euclid2026]

### Rubin Observatory

**Status:** surveying. Its ten-year Legacy Survey of Space and Time began with an announcement on 30 June 2026. From a mountaintop in Chile its 3,200-megapixel camera covers the whole southern sky every few nights, with dark energy and dark matter among its main targets.[^rubin2026]

### The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope

**Status:** launched on a Falcon Heavy from Florida on 30 August 2026 and now being commissioned; NASA expects its first science images by early 2027.[^roman2026] Dark energy is one of its three main science areas.[^romanmission]

### Gaia's fourth data release

**Status:** due on 2 December 2026.[^gaiadr4] Better parallaxes for Milky Way Cepheids firm up the bottom rung of the distance ladder, where a hidden error behind the Hubble tension could be lurking.

### The background radiation, again

**Status:** mixed. The Simons Observatory in Chile's Atacama Desert took its first image with its 6-metre Large Aperture Telescope in February 2025.[^so2025] CMB-S4, the planned next-generation American project, is over: on 9 July 2025 the Department of Energy and the National Science Foundation said they could no longer support it.[^cmbs4] Japan's LiteBIRD satellite would map the polarisation of the background over the whole sky, looking for traces of cosmic inflation, a proposed burst of extremely fast expansion at the very start. In June 2026 it passed a mission definition review that clears it to move into its preparation phase, and it aims to launch in Japan's fiscal year 2036, a date far enough off to move.[^litebird] NASA's SPHEREx, launched on 11 March 2025, is surveying more than 450 million galaxies to study the universe's origins.[^spherex]

### Open questions

The biggest is what dark energy is, and whether it is constant, like Einstein's Λ, or slowly fading, as DESI's hints suggest. Then there is the Hubble tension, and behind both, the question of what set the expansion going in the first place.
